«Gone with the Wind»: the story of an impossible shoot

8 December 2026 · 6 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

Three directors, hundreds of would-be Scarletts and eight Oscars: the 1939 epic that premiered in December.

On 15 December 1939 Gone with the Wind premiered in Atlanta. The governor declared a holiday, and thousands of people filled the streets to see the stars arrive. It was the climax of one of the most complicated productions in Hollywood history.

A best-selling novel and an obsessed producer

The film adapted the novel by Margaret Mitchell, published in 1936, which won the Pulitzer Prize and sold millions of copies. The producer David O. Selznick bought the rights and threw himself into the project with an almost unhealthy obsession: he wrote endless memos about every detail.

The search for Scarlett

Finding the actress to play Scarlett O'Hara became a national event. Dozens of famous actresses were screen-tested and hundreds of hopefuls were interviewed. When filming had already begun, with the burning of Atlanta, the British actress Vivien Leigh, almost unknown in America, appeared on the scene. Selznick was convinced as soon as he saw her, and the part was hers. The male lead, Rhett Butler, could only be one man: Clark Gable.

Three directors

The first director was George Cukor, who was fired after a few weeks. He was replaced by Victor Fleming, who became so exhausted that he had to take a break, during which Sam Wood took over. The screenplay was credited to Sidney Howard, although many other hands rewrote it, including Ben Hecht.

Eight Oscars and a pioneering actress

The film was an unprecedented hit and won eight competitive Oscars, including Best Picture, Director and Actress. Hattie McDaniel, who played Mammy, became the first African American to win an Oscar. Yet she was not even allowed to sit with her co-stars at the ceremony, because of the racial segregation of the time.

Today the film is also seen with a critical eye for its idealised view of the slave-owning South. Even so, adjusted for inflation, it remains one of the highest-grossing films of all time.
