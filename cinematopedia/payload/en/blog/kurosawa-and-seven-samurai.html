Kurosawa and «Seven Samurai»

19 November 2026 · 6 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

The 1954 film that invented the team of heroes gathered for a mission, and that Hollywood copied many times.

In 1954 a film almost three and a half hours long opened in Japan. It told the story of a farming village that hires seven masterless samurai to defend it from bandits. It was Seven Samurai, by Akira Kurosawa, and with it came one of the most copied formulas in cinema: the team of specialists who come together for an almost impossible mission.

The emperor of Japanese cinema

Kurosawa was born in Tokyo in 1910. He wanted to be a painter before he became a film-maker, and it shows in the composition of his shots. He was nicknamed «the Emperor» for his perfectionism on set. With Rashomon (1950), which won the Golden Lion in Venice in 1951, he surprised the West and opened the doors of Japanese cinema to the rest of the world.

A shoot in the rain

Seven Samurai was one of the most expensive Japanese productions of its time. The shoot went on for months, and the great final battle was filmed in torrential rain and deep mud. Kurosawa shot with several cameras at once, which was unusual then, to capture the action from different angles and cut it together at a breathless pace.

The cast was led by Takashi Shimura, as the wise leader Kambei, and Toshirō Mifune, as the impulsive Kikuchiyo, the fake samurai who is really a farmer's son. Mifune was Kurosawa's favourite actor for almost two decades.

From Japan to the West

Hollywood soon saw the story's potential. In 1960 John Sturges turned it into a western, The Magnificent Seven, with Yul Brynner and Steve McQueen. And it was not the only one: Yojimbo inspired Sergio Leone's A Fistful of Dollars, and George Lucas has said that The Hidden Fortress influenced him when he created Star Wars.

A legacy that does not age

Kurosawa kept working into the nineties, with films such as Ikiru, Dersu Uzala, Kagemusha and Ran. In 1990 he received an honorary Oscar. Seven Samurai regularly appears among the greatest films ever made, and seventy years on it is still surprisingly modern.
