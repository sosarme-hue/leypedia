Ramón y Cajal and the neuron

12 January 2027 · 6 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

How a Spanish doctor, with a microscope and great patience, discovered how the brain is built.

At the end of the 19th century nobody knew what the brain was made of. A Spanish doctor, with a microscope, a borrowed technique and endless patience, found the answer: the nervous system is made of independent cells, neurons. His name was Santiago Ramón y Cajal, and he is the greatest scientist in Spain's history.

A rebellious boy

Cajal was born in 1852 in Petilla de Aragón, a small Navarrese village surrounded by Aragonese land, and grew up in Aragon. As a boy he was rebellious and mischievous: he once built a small cannon and spent a few days in the village lock-up. He wanted to be a painter, but his father, a doctor, made him study medicine in Zaragoza. He served as an army doctor in Cuba, where he caught malaria.

The black reaction

In 1887, in Madrid, he came across a staining technique invented by the Italian Camillo Golgi, the «black reaction», which stained a few cells of nerve tissue black. Cajal improved it and applied it tirelessly to the brains of embryos and young animals, where cells were easier to see. He worked at home, with a microscope bought with his savings.

The neuron doctrine

Golgi and most scientists believed the nervous system was a continuous network, like a mesh of pipes. Cajal showed it was made of separate cells, neurons, which communicate by contact at specific points, later called synapses. He also described that the nerve impulse travels in one direction within each neuron. He presented his results in Berlin in 1889, and the leading German scientists were convinced.

A Nobel shared with his rival

In 1906 Cajal and Golgi shared the Nobel Prize in Medicine. At the ceremony in Stockholm, Golgi defended his network theory and attacked Cajal's. Time, and the electron microscope, proved the Spaniard right.

The artist of neurons

Cajal drew thousands of preparations by hand, and his drawings are as precise as they are beautiful; today they are exhibited in museums around the world. He called neurons «the mysterious butterflies of the soul». He was also a photographer and wrote a book of advice for young researchers that is still read. He died in Madrid in 1934.
