Isaac Newton, born on Christmas Day

22 December 2026 · 6 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

The man this site is named after was born on 25 December 1642. A look at his life and his great ideas.

On Christmas Day 1642, on a farm at Woolsthorpe in England, a baby was born so small that, his mother would say, he could have fitted into a quart pot. His name was Isaac Newton, and he would become one of the greatest scientists in history. This site is named after him.

A tricky date

Newton was born on 25 December 1642 by the Julian calendar, which England still used. In the Gregorian calendar, ours, the date is 4 January 1643. His father had died three months earlier, and his mother left him with his grandmother when she remarried.

The plague years

Newton studied at Cambridge, but in 1665 the plague closed the university and he went back to Woolsthorpe. In about eighteen months, barely in his twenties, he laid the foundations of calculus, discovered that white light is made of colours and began to think about gravity. He later said that a falling apple made him wonder whether the force pulling the fruit reached as far as the Moon.

The Principia

In 1687, encouraged and funded by the astronomer Edmond Halley, he published the Principia. In it he set out the three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation: the same force explains a falling apple, the tides and the orbits of the planets. It is one of the most influential works in the history of science.

Much more than gravity

Newton built the first practical reflecting telescope, using mirrors instead of lenses, and in 1704 published his Opticks. He was also a difficult man who waged bitter disputes, such as the one with Leibniz over who had invented calculus. He spent many years on alchemy and theology, presided over the Royal Society and ran the Royal Mint, where he hunted down counterfeiters.

On the shoulders of giants

He died in 1727 and was buried in Westminster Abbey. In a letter he wrote that if he had seen further, it was by standing on the shoulders of giants. Einstein's relativity corrected his theory two centuries later, but Newton's laws are still used to launch rockets and build bridges.
