Marie Curie and her two Nobel Prizes

19 November 2026 · 6 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

The first person to win two Nobel Prizes, and the only one in two different sciences.

She arrived in Paris at 24, with almost no money, to study at a university with hardly any women. She ended up as the first person to win two Nobel Prizes, and is still the only one to have won them in two different sciences. This is the story of Marie Curie.

From Warsaw to the Sorbonne

Maria Skłodowska was born in Warsaw in 1867, when Poland was partitioned and the Russian part barred women from university. She worked for years as a governess to pay for her sister's studies in Paris, and in 1891 she went too. She studied physics and mathematics at the Sorbonne, where she met Pierre Curie, whom she married in 1895.

Mysterious rays

In 1896 Henri Becquerel discovered that uranium salts gave off an invisible radiation. Marie chose to study it for her thesis and found that its intensity depended only on the amount of uranium: the radiation came from the atoms themselves. She coined the word radioactivity. With Pierre she worked for years in a freezing shed, processing tonnes of ore, and in 1898 they announced two new elements: polonium, named after Poland, and radium.

Two Nobel Prizes

In 1903 the Curies and Becquerel shared the Nobel Prize in Physics; Marie was the first woman to win one. In 1906 Pierre was killed by a horse-drawn cart on a Paris street. Marie took over his chair and became the first woman professor at the Sorbonne. In 1911 she won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry on her own for the discovery of radium and polonium.

The «little Curies»

During the First World War she set up mobile X-ray units to treat the wounded at the front, which soldiers called «petites Curies». She learned to drive to take them there herself, with her daughter Irène.

Her legacy

Marie Curie died in 1934 of a blood disease, probably caused by radiation. Her notebooks are still radioactive and are consulted with protection. Her daughter Irène Joliot-Curie also won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry, in 1935. In 1995 her remains were moved to the Panthéon in Paris; she was the first woman buried there on her own merits.
