Einstein and general relativity: gravity as curvature

19 November 2026 · 6 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

In November 1915 Einstein presented the theory that changed our idea of space, time and gravity.

On 25 November 1915, at a session of the Prussian Academy of Sciences in Berlin, Albert Einstein presented the equations of a theory that changed our idea of the universe: general relativity. More than a century later, we keep finding that he was right.

From special to general relativity

In 1905, his «miracle year», Einstein had published special relativity, which showed that time and space are not absolute: they depend on how the person measuring them is moving. But that theory left out gravity. For ten years Einstein looked for a way to bring the two together, through many mistakes and dead ends.

Gravity as curvature

The key idea is simple to tell and hard to calculate. For Newton, gravity was a force acting at a distance. For Einstein, matter and energy curve spacetime, and bodies move by following that curvature. A common image is a stretched sheet with a heavy ball in the middle: marbles rolling nearby curve towards it.

The first tests

The theory at once explained an anomaly Newton's physics could not solve: a small mismatch in the orbit of Mercury. In 1919, during a total solar eclipse, two British expeditions measured how starlight bent as it passed near the Sun, and the result matched Einstein's prediction. Newspapers around the world turned him into a celebrity.

Black holes, waves and GPS

General relativity predicts phenomena Einstein himself viewed sceptically, such as black holes. In 2015 gravitational waves, which he had predicted a century earlier, were detected for the first time, and in 2019 the first image of a black hole was published. And there is an everyday application: GPS satellites must correct their clocks for relativity, or they would drift by several kilometres a day.

A Nobel for something else

Curiously, Einstein received the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics not for relativity, which was still debated, but for his explanation of the photoelectric effect.
