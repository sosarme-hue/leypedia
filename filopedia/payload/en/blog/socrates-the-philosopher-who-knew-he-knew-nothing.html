Socrates, the philosopher who knew that he knew nothing

19 November 2026 · 5 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

Today is World Philosophy Day. We celebrate it with the Athenian who never wrote a book and changed thought for ever.

Every third Thursday of November UNESCO celebrates World Philosophy Day, and this year it falls today, 19 November. Few figures stand for that celebration better than Socrates, an Athenian who never wrote a single line and who nevertheless sits at the origin of almost all Western philosophy.

A philosopher of the street

Socrates was born around 469 BC in Athens. His father was a sculptor or stonemason and his mother, Phaenarete, a midwife. He liked to joke that he had inherited his mother's trade: he did not give birth to ideas of his own, but helped others to deliver theirs. That is why his method is called maieutics, Greek for the midwife's art.

He had no school and charged nothing for teaching, unlike the sophists of his day. He spent his days in the agora, the public square, asking politicians, poets and craftsmen what justice, courage or virtue were. His questions usually ended by showing that the other person did not know what they thought they knew.

«I know that I know nothing»

According to Plato, a friend of Socrates asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than him, and the answer was no. Puzzled, Socrates set out to find someone wiser and reached a conclusion: he was the wisest only because he was aware of his own ignorance. That is the origin of the famous saying, although he never put it in exactly those words.

Not everyone liked his way of asking questions. The comic playwright Aristophanes mocked him in «The Clouds», where he appears as a charlatan hanging in a basket.

The trial and the hemlock

In 399 BC he was accused of not believing in the city's gods and of corrupting the young. A jury of about five hundred citizens sentenced him to death. Plato recorded his defence in the Apology of Socrates. His friends offered to help him escape, as told in the Crito, but he refused: he had lived under the laws of Athens and would not break them now. He drank the hemlock surrounded by his followers, a scene Plato describes in the Phaedo.

Why he still matters

Everything we know about Socrates comes to us through others, above all Plato. Even so, his way of thinking is still alive: ask before you assert, examine your own beliefs and accept that you do not know everything. He summed it up in an idea that fits any age: the unexamined life is not worth living.
