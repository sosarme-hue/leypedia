Hannah Arendt and the banality of evil

12 January 2027 · 6 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

The philosopher who attended the Eichmann trial and found that the most terrible evil can come from ordinary people.

In 1961 the philosopher Hannah Arendt travelled to Jerusalem to report on the trial of Adolf Eichmann, one of the organisers of the deportation of millions of Jews to the Nazi death camps. She expected to find a monster. What she saw was a grey official who spoke in clichés. From that experience came one of the most debated ideas of the twentieth century: the banality of evil.

From Germany to New York

Arendt was born in 1906 near Hanover and grew up in Königsberg, the city of Kant. She studied philosophy with Martin Heidegger and wrote her doctorate under Karl Jaspers. In 1933, when the Nazis came to power, she was arrested by the Gestapo for researching antisemitic propaganda. She was released after a few days and fled to Paris.

In 1940 she was interned in the camp at Gurs, in southern France, and managed to escape. The following year she reached New York, where she lived for the rest of her life. For years she was stateless, an experience that shaped her thinking.

Thinking about totalitarianism

In 1951 she published The Origins of Totalitarianism, a study of Nazism and Stalinism. For Arendt, totalitarianism was something new in history: it did not seek merely to govern, but to control people's lives completely, and it used terror and ideology to do so. In The Human Condition (1958) she reflected on labour, action and public life.

The banality of evil

Her book Eichmann in Jerusalem was published in 1963 and caused a huge controversy. Arendt argued that Eichmann was neither a sadist nor a fanatic, but someone unable to think for himself, who obeyed orders and wanted to get ahead in his career. The most terrible evil, she concluded, does not need great villains: it only takes people who stop thinking about what they are doing.

Many attacked her fiercely and some friends stopped speaking to her. She held her ground. She died in New York in 1975, and her call to think for oneself is still very much alive.
