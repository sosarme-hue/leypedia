It sounds contradictory, but it is true: in early January, in the middle of the northern winter, Earth is closer to the Sun than at any other time of year. This year it happens around 3 January. That point in the orbit is called perihelion.

{fig:img/universo/sol.jpg|universe/solar-system/sun/|The Sun in extreme ultraviolet light, captured by NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory. Image: NASA/SDO · Public domain}

An almost circular orbit

Earth travels around the Sun along an ellipse, but a very slightly flattened one: almost a circle. At perihelion we are about 147 million kilometres from the Sun, and at aphelion, in early July, about 152 million. The difference is about five million kilometres, just over 3%.

So why is it cold?

Because the seasons depend not on our distance from the Sun but on the tilt of Earth's axis. In January the northern hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun, the rays arrive at a lower angle and there are fewer hours of daylight. That is why it is winter even though we are closer. The proof is that, at the same time, it is high summer in the southern hemisphere.

It is one of the most widespread myths about space. You will find others just as surprising on our {u:universe/curiosities/myths/|space myths} page.

A slightly shorter winter

Being closer does have one curious effect. When it is nearer the Sun, Earth moves faster along its orbit, at about 30 kilometres per second. That is why the astronomical winter in the northern hemisphere lasts about 89 days, while summer lasts about 94.

In addition, in January the whole Earth receives about 7% more solar energy than in July. The effect is spread out by the oceans and we hardly notice it, but climate scientists take it into account.

It hasn't always been this way

Earth's orbit and axis change slowly over tens of thousands of years. In about 10,000 years perihelion will fall in the northern summer. These cycles, called Milankovitch cycles, help explain the ice ages of the past. Find out more about our planet in {u:universe/solar-system/earth/|Earth in the solar system}.
