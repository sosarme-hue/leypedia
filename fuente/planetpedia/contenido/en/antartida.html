It is the coldest, driest, windiest and, on average, highest continent on the planet. It has no permanent inhabitants, yet it holds almost all of Earth's fresh water and is key to understanding the climate. We travel to Antarctica, now that it is summer there.

A continent under ice

Antarctica covers about 14 million square kilometres, more than three times the area of the European Union. Ninety-eight per cent of it is covered by an ice sheet almost two kilometres thick on average, and more than 4,500 metres thick in places. That ice holds about 70% of the planet's fresh water. If it all melted, sea level would rise by about 58 metres.

Beneath the ice there are mountains, valleys and more than 400 lakes of liquid water, such as the huge Lake Vostok, cut off from the outside world for millions of years.

The race to the South Pole

On 14 December 1911 the Norwegian Roald Amundsen and his team were the first to reach the South Pole. The British explorer Robert Falcon Scott arrived on 17 January 1912 and found the Norwegian tent there. Scott and his companions died on the return journey, trapped by cold and storms.

A continent for science

In 1959 twelve countries signed the Antarctic Treaty, which reserves the continent for peaceful and scientific purposes and bans military activity. Today more than fifty countries have signed it. Spain has two research stations, Juan Carlos I and Gabriel de Castilla, which operate during the southern summer, from December to March.

Scientists drill out columns of ice, called ice cores, which hold air bubbles hundreds of thousands of years old. Thanks to them we know how the amount of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere has changed over time.

Life in extreme cold

Despite the conditions, the coast and the Southern Ocean teem with life: penguins, seals, whales and krill, a small crustacean that is the basis of the whole food chain. The emperor penguin breeds in the depths of winter, at temperatures of −40 °C.
