On 21 December the winter solstice arrives in the northern hemisphere. It is the day with the fewest hours of daylight of the year and, astronomically speaking, the start of winter. From then on the days slowly begin to lengthen.

It's all down to the tilt

Earth's axis of rotation is not upright: it is tilted about 23.4 degrees relative to its orbit around the Sun. So over the year each hemisphere receives the Sun's rays more directly or more obliquely. In December the northern hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun: the rays arrive at a lower angle, warm less, and the Sun traces a shorter, lower arc across the sky.

The seasons are not caused by being nearer to or farther from the Sun. In fact, in early January Earth is at the point in its orbit closest to it.

The shortest day, but not the earliest sunset

In London the solstice brings less than eight hours of daylight, and in Madrid about nine and a quarter. But here is a curiosity: the earliest sunset is not on the solstice but some days before, in early or mid-December, and the latest sunrise comes in early January. This is because Earth's orbit is not a perfect circle and because of the tilt of its axis, which together make solar noon shift slightly each day.

Summer on the other side of the world

While winter begins in Europe, summer starts in Argentina, Chile or Australia, where it is the longest day of the year. In Antarctica the Sun does not set at all, and at the North Pole it has been dark for weeks.

A very old celebration

Many cultures have celebrated the winter solstice as the rebirth of the Sun. Monuments such as Newgrange in Ireland, built more than 5,000 years ago, are aligned so that the light of the solstice sunrise shines into their interior.
