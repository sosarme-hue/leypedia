On 13 December 2021 the volcano on La Palma stopped spewing lava. It had been 85 days since, on 19 September, a fissure opened on the slopes of the Cumbre Vieja ridge. Five years on, the Tajogaite eruption remains the most closely studied in Spain's history and a lesson in the power of our planet.

An island born of volcanoes

The Canary Islands are volcanic, and La Palma is one of the youngest and most active. Before 2021 it had erupted in 1949 and in 1971, when the Teneguía volcano formed. In the weeks before, seismographs recorded thousands of small earthquakes and the ground rose by several centimetres: magma was on its way up.

85 days of eruption

The eruption began on the afternoon of 19 September. For almost three months the volcano threw out lava, ash and gas. The lava flows advanced towards the sea through the Aridane valley and covered more than 1,200 hectares, burying whole neighbourhoods, roads, banana plantations and thousands of buildings. Some 7,000 people had to leave their homes.

When the lava reached the ocean it built new rock platforms, known as lava deltas, which made the island grow. Ash covered rooftops and forced the airport to close several times. Thanks to monitoring and timely evacuations, the lava caused no direct deaths.

What we learned

Scientists from around the world followed the eruption with seismographs, satellites, drones and gas analysis. A great deal was learned about how magma rises, how lava flows advance and how to predict their path. It also became clear that the danger does not end when the volcano goes quiet: for a long time, carbon dioxide seeping from the ground built up in coastal areas such as Puerto Naos and La Bombilla, and residents had to wait a long time before they could return.

Five years later

Rebuilding has been slow, and some families have still not got their homes back. New roads have been laid across the cooled lava, and the Tajogaite cone has become a landscape that reminds everyone that the Canaries are a land of living volcanoes.
