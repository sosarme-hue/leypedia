On Christmas Eve 1968, three astronauts became the first human beings to see the far side of the Moon and to look back at Earth from afar. The mission was Apollo 8, and it produced one of the most famous photographs in history.

{fig:img/universo/salida_tierra.jpg|universe/space-exploration/apollo-and-the-moon/|«Earthrise», the photo William Anders took from lunar orbit on 24 December 1968. Image: NASA · Public domain}

A mission brought forward

At the height of the {u:universe/space-exploration/space-race/|space race}, NASA decided to speed up its programme and send a crewed spacecraft around the Moon earlier than planned. On 21 December 1968 a huge Saturn V rocket lifted off from Florida with Frank Borman, Jim Lovell and William Anders on board. It was the first time that rocket had carried astronauts.

Ten orbits of the Moon

After almost three days of travel, on 24 December the spacecraft entered lunar orbit. As they passed behind the Moon they lost radio contact with Earth: they were more alone than any humans had ever been. They circled the Moon ten times, photographed its surface and studied possible landing sites for the missions that followed.

«Earthrise»

On the fourth orbit, as they came round the lunar horizon, they saw Earth appear: a blue and white sphere above the grey landscape of the Moon. Anders grabbed the camera and took the picture we now know as «Earthrise». Many believe that this image of a small, fragile planet helped inspire the environmental movement in the years that followed.

That same night, in a broadcast watched by hundreds of millions of people, the astronauts read the opening verses of Genesis and wished a merry Christmas to everyone on Earth.

The road to the first step

The crew splashed down in the Pacific on 27 December. Seven months later, Apollo 11 took the first astronauts to walk on the Moon.
