On 24 November 1859 a book with a very long title went on sale in London: «On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection». Its author, Charles Darwin, had been mulling it over for more than twenty years. That book changed for ever the way we understand life on Earth.

The voyage of the Beagle

Darwin was born in England in 1809. At 22 he sailed as a naturalist on HMS Beagle, a Royal Navy ship that travelled around the world between 1831 and 1836. During the voyage he collected fossils, plants and animals, and studied landscapes in South America, Australia and many islands.

On the Galápagos Islands, in the Pacific Ocean, he noticed that each island had its own giant tortoises and its own finches, with different beaks depending on what they ate. Why would there be such similar but different species on islands so close together?

Natural selection

Back in England, Darwin arrived at a simple and powerful idea. Individuals of the same species are not identical, and some of their differences are inherited. Because more individuals are born than can survive, those with traits better suited to their environment live longer and leave more offspring. Over many generations these small changes add up and can give rise to new species. This is {u:earth/life-and-ecosystems/evolution/|evolution} by natural selection.

An unexpected letter

Darwin was in no hurry to publish, but in 1858 he received a letter from another naturalist, Alfred Russel Wallace, who had independently reached the same idea. Their two theories were presented together in London that same year, and Darwin finally finished his book. The first edition sold out to booksellers on the day it came out.

A tree of life

The theory caused great debate, because it contradicted the idea that species had been created just as they are. Today it is confirmed by fossils, comparative anatomy and genetics, and it is the foundation of all biology. Darwin died in 1882 and is buried in Westminster Abbey.
