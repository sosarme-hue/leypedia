On 8 November 1895, in his laboratory in Würzburg, Germany, the physicist Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen was experimenting with a cathode-ray tube covered in black cardboard. Suddenly he saw that a screen coated with a fluorescent substance, some distance away, was glowing in the dark. Something invisible was passing through the cardboard. He called it X-rays, because he did not know what they were.

Weeks shut away in the laboratory

Röntgen spent the following weeks studying those rays, hardly leaving the laboratory. He found that they passed through paper, wood and skin, but not through metal or bone, and that they left an image on photographic plates.

Anna Bertha's hand

On 22 December 1895 he asked his wife, Anna Bertha, to place her hand on a photographic plate and exposed it to the rays for several minutes. The image showed the bones of her fingers and her wedding ring. According to the story, when she saw it she exclaimed that she had seen her own death. It was the first X-ray image of part of a human body.

News that spread like wildfire

Röntgen published his discovery at the end of December, and by January 1896 the news was in newspapers all over the world. Within weeks, doctors in many countries were using X-rays to find fractures and bullets. Röntgen refused to patent his discovery, because he believed it should benefit everyone. In 1901 he received the first Nobel Prize in Physics ever awarded. You can read more on our page about the {a:descubrimiento-de-los-rayos-x|discovery of X-rays}.

The risks of the early years

At first nobody knew the dangers of radiation. Many of the first radiologists suffered burns and cancers from repeated exposure, and people had to learn to protect themselves with lead and to reduce the doses. Today X-rays are still one of the basic tools of diagnosis, alongside techniques that grew out of them, such as CT scanning.
