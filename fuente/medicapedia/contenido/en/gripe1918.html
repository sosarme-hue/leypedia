Between 1918 and 1920 a flu travelled around the world in several waves and killed, according to estimates, between 25 and 50 million people, perhaps more. It was the {d:pandemia-de-gripe-de-1918|deadliest pandemic of the twentieth century}, and it arrived just as the world was emerging from the First World War.

Why «Spanish»?

The flu did not start in Spain. Its exact origin is still unknown, although some of the first recorded outbreaks were in US army camps in the spring of 1918. But the countries at war censored the news so as not to demoralise their people, while the press in neutral Spain reported freely. When King Alfonso XIII himself fell ill, the news went round the world, and the disease became known as the «Spanish flu».

A flu that killed the young

The most puzzling thing about that flu is that it hit young, healthy adults aged 20 to 40 hard, not just children and the elderly like ordinary flu. Many died of pneumonia within days. The second wave, in the autumn of 1918, was the deadliest. Troop movements, ships and trains helped the virus reach almost every corner of the planet.

No vaccines, no antibiotics

Doctors at the time did not know that flu was caused by a virus, which was not identified until 1933. There were no vaccines, and no antibiotics to treat the pneumonias that complicated it. The weapons were the same as in earlier epidemics: closing schools and theatres, banning gatherings, gauze masks and isolating the sick. Cities that acted earlier and kept their measures longer generally had fewer deaths.

What we learned

In 2005 a team of scientists reconstructed the virus from preserved samples and confirmed that it was an influenza A virus of the H1N1 subtype. Descendants of that virus kept circulating for decades. The experience of 1918 is still used today to prepare pandemic plans.
