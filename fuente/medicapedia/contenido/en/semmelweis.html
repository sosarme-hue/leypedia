Today it seems obvious that a doctor should wash their hands before seeing a patient. In 1847 it was not. The doctor who proved it, {p:ignaz-semmelweis|Ignaz Semmelweis}, was ignored and ridiculed by many of his colleagues, and died without seeing his discovery recognised.

{fig:img/p/ignaz-semmelweis.jpg|p/ignaz-semmelweis.html|Ignaz Semmelweis. Image: Wikimedia Commons.}

Two wards, two fates

Semmelweis was born in 1818 in Buda, now part of Budapest, and worked at the maternity unit of Vienna General Hospital. It had two delivery wards. In the first, women were attended by doctors and medical students; in the second, by midwives. Far more mothers died in the first from childbed fever, an infection that appeared after giving birth. The women knew it, and some begged not to be admitted there.

The death of a friend

In 1847 his colleague Jakob Kolletschka died after cutting himself with a scalpel during a post-mortem. His symptoms were very similar to those of the mothers. Semmelweis reached a conclusion: doctors and students went from the dissecting room to the delivery ward carrying «cadaveric particles» on their hands, which infected the women in labour. The midwives did not perform post-mortems.

Chlorinated water

Semmelweis ordered everyone entering the delivery ward to wash their hands in a solution of chlorinated lime. Mortality fell dramatically, to levels similar to those of the midwives' ward. But many doctors were offended by the idea that they themselves were spreading death, and germ theory did not yet exist to explain why it worked.

A sad end and a late vindication

Semmelweis lost his post in Vienna, returned to Hungary and went on defending his ideas in increasingly angry letters. In 1865 he was committed to an asylum, where he died within weeks. Years later, the work of {p:louis-pasteur|Louis Pasteur} and {p:joseph-lister|Joseph Lister} showed he had been right. Today he is remembered as the «saviour of mothers», and hand hygiene is one of the most effective ways to prevent infections in hospitals.
