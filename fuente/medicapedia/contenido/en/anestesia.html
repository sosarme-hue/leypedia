Until the mid-nineteenth century, having an operation was a terrifying experience. Patients were awake, held down by several assistants, and surgeons prided themselves on their speed: the shorter the operation, the less the patient suffered. Everything changed within a few years, in the 1840s, with the discovery of anaesthesia.

{fig:img/p/william-morton.jpg|p/william-morton.html|William Morton. Image: Wikimedia Commons.}

Laughing gas

Nitrous oxide, known as laughing gas, was used at shows and parties because it made people euphoric. In 1844 a dentist from Hartford, {p:horace-wells|Horace Wells}, noticed that a man who had inhaled it injured his leg and felt nothing. The next day Wells had one of his own teeth pulled under its effects without feeling any pain. But when he tried to demonstrate it in public in Boston, the patient cried out and the demonstration was judged a failure.

Ether and 16 October 1846

A former partner of Wells, the dentist {p:william-morton|William Morton}, tried another substance: ether. On 16 October 1846, at the Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston, Morton anaesthetised a patient, Gilbert Abbott, with ether while the surgeon John Collins Warren removed a tumour from his neck. When it was over, Warren told the audience that this was no humbug. The room where it happened is known today as the Ether Dome. Chloroform and Queen Victoria

The news crossed the Atlantic within weeks. In 1847 the Scottish obstetrician {p:james-young-simpson|James Young Simpson} began using chloroform, which was easier to give than ether. In 1853 the physician {p:john-snow|John Snow} gave it to Queen Victoria during the birth of her son Leopold, and royal approval helped make anaesthesia in childbirth acceptable.

A new kind of surgery

Anaesthesia allowed surgeons to work calmly and to attempt long, complex operations that had been impossible before. A few decades later {p:joseph-lister|Joseph Lister}'s antisepsis cut infections, and together they transformed surgery. Today anaesthesiology is a medical specialty in its own right, and it is hard to imagine medicine without it.
