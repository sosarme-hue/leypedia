On 6 July 1885 a nine-year-old boy from Alsace, Joseph Meister, was brought to {p:louis-pasteur|Louis Pasteur}'s laboratory in Paris. Two days earlier he had been bitten several times by a rabid dog. Once symptoms appeared, rabies meant certain death. Pasteur had an experimental vaccine that he had only tried on dogs.

{fig:img/p/louis-pasteur.jpg|p/louis-pasteur.html|Louis Pasteur. Image: Wikimedia Commons.}

A chemist among microbes

Pasteur was born on 27 December 1822 in Dole, in France. He was not a doctor but a chemist. He had shown that fermentation and putrefaction were caused by micro-organisms, had developed pasteurisation to preserve wine and milk, and had created vaccines against chicken cholera and anthrax in livestock. Together with {p:robert-koch|Robert Koch}, he was one of the fathers of germ theory.

A vaccine against an invisible enemy

The cause of rabies, a virus, was too small to be seen with the microscopes of the time. Pasteur and his colleague {p:emile-roux|Émile Roux} managed to weaken it by drying the spinal cords of infected rabbits for days. By injecting cord that was less and less dried, dogs could be protected before the disease developed, since rabies takes weeks to appear after a bite.

Thirteen injections

Pasteur hesitated, because he was not a doctor and had never tried it on a person. After consulting two physicians, Joseph Meister received thirteen injections over ten days. The boy did not develop rabies. Months later Pasteur successfully treated a fifteen-year-old shepherd, Jean-Baptiste Jupille, who had been bitten while protecting other children from a rabid dog.

The Pasteur Institute

The news went round the world, and people who had been bitten came to Paris from many countries. Thanks to an international subscription, the Pasteur Institute was founded in 1888, and it is still one of the world's great research centres. Years later Joseph Meister worked as the institute's caretaker. Today you can read about the current {f:vacuna-contra-la-rabia|rabies vaccine} on our page.
