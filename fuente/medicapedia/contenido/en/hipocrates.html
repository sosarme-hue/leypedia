About 2,400 years ago, on the Greek island of Kos, lived a physician whose name medical students all over the world still speak today. {p:hipocrates|Hippocrates} is regarded as the father of Western medicine, although we know very little about his life.

{fig:img/p/hipocrates.jpg|p/hipocrates.html|Hippocrates, in an engraving by Peter Paul Rubens. Image: Wikimedia Commons.}

Medicine without gods

In ancient Greece it was common to think that illnesses were punishments or decisions of the gods, and many sick people went to the temples of Asclepius to seek a cure. The great contribution of the Hippocratic school was to look for natural causes: climate, water, diet, way of life. A famous treatise on epilepsy, the so-called «sacred disease», argued that it was no more divine than any other illness.

Observing the patient

Hippocratic doctors placed great importance on observing the patient, recording symptoms and following the course of the illness. They believed health depended on the balance between four humours of the body: blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile. That theory was wrong, but, developed later by {p:galeno|Galen}, it dominated medicine for almost two thousand years.

The Hippocratic Corpus

Some sixty treatises attributed to Hippocrates survive, the Hippocratic Corpus, although historians believe they were written by several authors of his school over decades. They contain descriptions of diseases, advice on diet, surgery and prognosis, and sayings that have become famous, such as «life is short, the art is long».

The oath

The best-known text is the Hippocratic Oath, in which the physician swears before the gods to act for the benefit of the sick, to do them no harm and to keep secret what they see and hear. Many medical schools still recite a modern version of that commitment at graduation, such as the World Medical Association's Declaration of Geneva. The principle «first, do no harm», although it does not appear in those words in the oath, sums up its spirit well.
