Few objects identify a doctor as clearly as a stethoscope round the neck. Its inventor was a French physician, {p:rene-laennec|René Laennec}, and its origin is almost an anecdote: in 1816 he rolled up some sheets of paper to listen to a patient's heart.

{fig:img/p/rene-laennec.jpg|p/rene-laennec.html|René Laennec. Image: Wikimedia Commons.}

Listening with an ear to the chest

Laennec was born in 1781 in Quimper, in Brittany. He trained in Paris with doctors such as {p:jean-nicolas-corvisart|Jean-Nicolas Corvisart}, who had revived a technique invented by the Viennese doctor {p:leopold-auenbrugger|Leopold Auenbrugger}: tapping the chest with the fingers to tell from the sound whether there was fluid or air inside. To listen to the heart or lungs, doctors pressed their ear directly against the patient's chest.

A roll of paper

In 1816 Laennec had to examine a young woman with heart trouble. Because of her age and out of modesty, he did not think it proper to put his ear to her chest. He then remembered that if you scratch one end of a wooden beam, the sound can be heard clearly at the other end. He rolled some sheets of paper into a tube, placed one end on the patient's chest and the other at his ear, and heard her heart more clearly than ever before.

From paper to wood

He then made a wooden tube about 25 centimetres long and called it the stethoscope, from the Greek words for «chest» and «to look at». But what mattered was not only the device: Laennec spent years listening to his patients and comparing the sounds with what he found at post-mortem. In this way he described and named many sounds of the lungs and heart. In 1819 he published it all in his Treatise on Mediate Auscultation.

An ironic end

Laennec devoted much of his work to tuberculosis, the disease that caused the most deaths in his time. He died of it in 1826, aged only 45. His invention evolved: in the mid-nineteenth century stethoscopes with two earpieces and flexible tubes appeared, the model we know today.
