Imagine prisoners chained since childhood at the back of a cave, always facing the wall. Behind them a fire is burning, and between the fire and the prisoners people pass by carrying objects. The prisoners see only the shadows of those objects on the wall and believe that is all there is. This is the allegory of the cave, the most famous image in philosophy, and {a:platon|Plato} wrote it almost 2,400 years ago.

The pupil of Socrates

Plato was born in Athens around 427 BC into an aristocratic family. As a young man he wanted a career in politics, but meeting {a:socrates|Socrates} changed his life. His teacher's death sentence in 399 convinced him that the city needed a different way of thinking about justice. Around 387 he founded the Academy in Athens, a school where philosophy and mathematics were studied and where {a:aristoteles|Aristotle} was trained.

He wrote almost all his work as dialogues, in which Socrates is usually the main character. Among them are the {o:el-banquete|Symposium}, on love, and the {o:la-republica|Republic}, on the just city.

Leaving the cave

The allegory appears in Book VII of the Republic. Plato imagines that one of the prisoners is set free. At first the fire dazzles him and he understands nothing. Then he is led out of the cave and, little by little, grows used to the light: first he sees reflections in water, then real things and, finally, the sun. Only then does he understand that what he saw before were mere shadows.

If he goes back into the cave to tell the others, nobody believes him. His eyes can no longer adjust to the dark, and the prisoners think going outside has ruined his sight. Plato hints that they would even try to kill him, as Athens did to Socrates.

What it means

The cave stands for the world we perceive through the senses, and the outside for the world of ideas, which can only be reached through reason. It lies at the heart of the {g:teoria-de-las-formas|theory of Forms} and of {c:platonismo|Platonism}. The sun symbolises the idea of the Good, which lights up everything else.

But the allegory also has a simple reading: what we take for granted may be only part of the truth, and learning means leaving our comfort zone. A good resolution for the start of the year.
