On 10 December 1830, {a:emily-dickinson|Emily Dickinson} was born in Amherst, a small town in Massachusetts. She wrote nearly 1,800 poems, but only about ten were published in her lifetime, almost all without her name. Today she is regarded as one of the great poets of the English language.

A life in the family home

Dickinson belonged to a prominent Amherst family: her grandfather was one of the founders of Amherst College, and her father was a lawyer and congressman. She studied at the local academy and spent a year at a female seminary, but came back home and stayed there. Over the years she went out less and less, received few visitors and spoke to some of them through a door. In town she was known as an eccentric woman who always dressed in white.

That withdrawn life was not an isolated one. She wrote hundreds of letters to friends, relatives and editors, and she read widely: the Bible, Shakespeare, the Brontës and the poets of her day.

Hand-sewn booklets

Dickinson copied her poems onto sheets of paper that she then sewed together by hand into small booklets. When she died in May 1886, her sister Lavinia found about forty of them in a drawer and decided to publish them. The first selection appeared in 1890, but the editors corrected the punctuation, changed words and added titles. Readers had to wait until 1955 to read the poems as she wrote them.

Dashes, capitals and eternity

Her poems are short and dense. She uses slant rhymes, unexpected capital letters and many dashes that make you read slowly. She writes about nature, love, God and, above all, death and eternity. In one of her best-known poems, Death stops to pick up the poet in a carriage and takes her for a drive with perfect courtesy.

Dickinson wrote that if reading a book made her feel physically as if the top of her head were taken off, she knew that was poetry. Many readers feel exactly that when they read her.
