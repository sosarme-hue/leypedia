On the night of 7 January 1610, in Padua, {p:galileo-galilei|Galileo Galilei} pointed a telescope he had built himself at Jupiter and saw, beside the planet, three small «stars» in a line. Over the following nights they moved, and soon a fourth appeared. He had discovered the first moons of another planet, and with them he cast doubt on the view of the universe inherited from Ptolemy.

{fig:img/p/Q307.webp|p/galileo-galilei.html|Galileo Galilei. Image: Wikimedia Commons.}

A Dutch invention

The {d:telescope|telescope} had been invented in the Netherlands in 1608. Galileo heard about it in 1609 and built his own, improving it until it magnified about twenty times. He pointed it at the sky and in a few months saw mountains and craters on the Moon, countless stars in the Milky Way and, at last, the moons of Jupiter.

The starry messenger

In March 1610 he published his discoveries in a short book, the Sidereus Nuncius, «the starry messenger». To win the favour of the Grand Duke of Tuscany, Cosimo II de' Medici, he called the moons the «Medicean stars». The book made him famous across Europe, and soon afterwards he became mathematician and philosopher to the court in Florence.

Why it mattered so much

In the tradition of Aristotle and Ptolemy, the Earth was the centre of the universe and everything revolved around it. Jupiter's moons showed that there were bodies orbiting another planet. This did not prove that the Earth went round the Sun, as Copernicus proposed, but it did show the old model was wrong. Soon afterwards Galileo observed the phases of Venus, more evidence in the same direction.

Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto

The names we use today were proposed by the German astronomer Simon Marius, who claimed to have seen them at almost the same time, following a suggestion by {p:johannes-kepler|Kepler}: they are lovers and companions of the god Jupiter in mythology. Today we call them the Galilean moons.

The trial and the legacy

His defence of Copernicus brought Galileo before the Inquisition, which condemned him in 1633. He spent his last years confined to his house near Florence, still doing research. He died in 1642, and a few months later {art:newton|Isaac Newton} was born in England. Today several space probes study those moons, especially Europa, which hides an ocean beneath its ice.
