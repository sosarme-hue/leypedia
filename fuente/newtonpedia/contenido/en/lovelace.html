In 1843, more than a hundred years before the first electronic computer, a young English mathematician wrote what is now considered the first computer program. Her name was {p:ada-lovelace|Ada Lovelace}, and she was the daughter of the poet Lord Byron.

{fig:img/p/Q7259.webp|p/ada-lovelace.html|Ada Lovelace. Image: Wikimedia Commons.}

The poet's daughter

Ada was born on 10 December 1815. Her parents separated when she was a baby and she never knew her father. Her mother, Annabella Milbanke, a keen mathematician, wanted to keep her away from Byron's poetic «madness» and gave her a scientific education very unusual for a girl at the time. Even so, Ada never gave up imagination: she spoke of a «poetical science».

Meeting Babbage

In 1833, aged 17, she met {p:charles-babbage|Charles Babbage}, a mathematician who had designed a large mechanical calculator, the {d:difference-engine|Difference Engine}. Babbage was already working on something even more ambitious, the {d:analytical-engine|Analytical Engine}: a machine that could follow instructions written on punched cards, like those of the {d:jacquard-loom|Jacquard loom}. It was never built, but on paper it had memory, a calculating unit and the ability to repeat operations, like a modern computer.

The 1843 notes

Ada translated from French an article on the Analytical Engine by the Italian engineer Luigi Menabrea and added notes of her own three times as long as the original. In the last one, Note G, she explained step by step how the machine could calculate {d:bernoulli-number|Bernoulli numbers}. It is the first published algorithm designed to be carried out by a machine.

Beyond numbers

The most striking thing about her notes is not the calculation but the vision. Ada saw that such a machine could handle not just numbers but any symbols, and might one day compose music. At the same time she warned that the machine does not «originate» anything itself: it only does what it is told. A century later Alan Turing discussed this idea in his famous paper on thinking machines.

Her legacy

Ada Lovelace died in 1852, aged just 36, the same age as her father. In 1980 the US Department of Defense named a programming language «Ada», and every year, on the second Tuesday of October, Ada Lovelace Day celebrates women in science.
