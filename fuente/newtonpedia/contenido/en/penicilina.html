In September 1928 the Scottish bacteriologist {p:alexander-fleming|Alexander Fleming} came back from holiday to his laboratory at St Mary's Hospital in London and found something odd on a forgotten culture plate. That slip would lead to the first antibiotic in history.

{fig:img/p/Q37064.webp|p/alexander-fleming.html|Alexander Fleming. Image: Wikimedia Commons.}

A mould on the plate

Fleming was studying staphylococci, bacteria that cause infections. A mould had grown on one of the plates, and around it the bacteria had disappeared. Fleming identified the mould as a Penicillium and called the substance it produced «penicillin». He published his results in 1929.

A difficult substance

The problem was that penicillin was very hard to purify and to produce in quantity, and Fleming lacked the means to do it. For a decade the discovery was almost forgotten.

The Oxford team

In 1939 a team at the University of Oxford led by {p:howard-florey|Howard Florey} and {p:ernst-chain|Ernst Chain} took up the work. In 1940 they showed that penicillin cured infected mice. In 1941 they tried it on a policeman with a severe infection, who improved dramatically; but the penicillin ran out and the patient died. It had to be produced on an industrial scale.

War and mass production

In the middle of the Second World War, laboratories and companies in the United States set to work. In Peoria, Illinois, a mouldy cantaloupe from a market yielded a strain that produced far more penicillin. By 1944 there was enough to treat the soldiers wounded in the Normandy landings. {d:benzylpenicillin|Penicillin} saved millions of lives.

The prize and a warning

In 1945 Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in Medicine. In his lecture Fleming warned that misusing penicillin could make bacteria resistant. Eighty years later, antibiotic resistance is one of medicine's great problems.
