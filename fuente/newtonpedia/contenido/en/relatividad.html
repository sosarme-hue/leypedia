On 25 November 1915, at a session of the Prussian Academy of Sciences in Berlin, {p:albert-einstein|Albert Einstein} presented the equations of a theory that changed our idea of the universe: {d:general-relativity|general relativity}. More than a century later, we keep finding that he was right.

{fig:img/p/Q937.webp|p/albert-einstein.html|Albert Einstein. Image: Wikimedia Commons.}

From special to general relativity

In 1905, his «miracle year», Einstein had published {d:special-relativity|special relativity}, which showed that time and space are not absolute: they depend on how the person measuring them is moving. But that theory left out gravity. For ten years Einstein looked for a way to bring the two together, through many mistakes and dead ends.

Gravity as curvature

The key idea is simple to tell and hard to calculate. For {p:isaac-newton|Newton}, {d:gravity|gravity} was a force acting at a distance. For Einstein, matter and energy curve spacetime, and bodies move by following that curvature. A common image is a stretched sheet with a heavy ball in the middle: marbles rolling nearby curve towards it.

The first tests

The theory at once explained an anomaly Newton's physics could not solve: a small mismatch in the orbit of Mercury. In 1919, during a total solar eclipse, two British expeditions measured how starlight bent as it passed near the Sun, and the result matched Einstein's prediction. Newspapers around the world turned him into a celebrity.

Black holes, waves and GPS

General relativity predicts phenomena Einstein himself viewed sceptically, such as {d:black-hole|black holes}. In 2015 {d:gravitational-wave|gravitational waves}, which he had predicted a century earlier, were detected for the first time, and in 2019 the first image of a black hole was published. And there is an everyday application: {d:global-positioning-system|GPS} satellites must correct their clocks for relativity, or they would drift by several kilometres a day.

A Nobel for something else

Curiously, Einstein received the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics not for relativity, which was still debated, but for his explanation of the {d:photoelectric-effect|photoelectric effect}.
