Every 10 December, in Stockholm and Oslo, the Nobel Prizes are awarded, the most famous prizes in the world. Behind them lies a curious story: that of a Swedish chemist who grew rich from explosives and wanted his fortune to reward those who had done most for humanity.

{fig:img/p/Q23810.webp|p/alfred-nobel.html|Alfred Nobel. Image: Wikimedia Commons.}

The inventor of dynamite

{p:alfred-nobel|Alfred Nobel} was born in Stockholm in 1833. He worked with nitroglycerine, a very powerful but very unstable explosive; an accident at the family factory killed his younger brother, Emil. Looking for a safe way to handle it, in 1867 he mixed it with a porous earth and obtained {d:dynamite|dynamite}. It was a worldwide success in mines, tunnels and public works, but also in war. Nobel held more than 350 patents and factories in some twenty countries.

A mistaken obituary

The story goes that in 1888, when his brother Ludvig died, a French newspaper thought it was Alfred and published his obituary under a terrible headline: «The merchant of death is dead». According to the story, Nobel worried about how he would be remembered. True or not, in 1895 he signed a will that surprised everyone.

The will

Nobel left most of his fortune to create annual prizes in five fields: Physics, Chemistry, Physiology or Medicine, Literature and Peace. He died on 10 December 1896, which is why the prizes are awarded on that day. The first were given in 1901: the Physics prize went to Wilhelm Röntgen, discoverer of {d:x-ray|X-rays}. The Peace Prize is awarded in Oslo, because Norway and Sweden were then united and Nobel wanted a Norwegian committee to decide it. In 1968 Sweden's central bank also created a prize in Economics in his memory.

Records and curiosities

{art:curie|Marie Curie} was the first person to win two Nobel Prizes. The Curie family has five in all: two for Marie, one for Pierre, and those of their daughter Irène and son-in-law Frédéric Joliot. The youngest laureate is Malala Yousafzai, who won the Peace Prize at 17.

Science in Spanish

Spanish-speaking science laureates include {p:santiago-ramon-y-cajal|Santiago Ramón y Cajal} (Medicine, 1906), Bernardo Houssay (1947), {p:severo-ochoa|Severo Ochoa} (1959), Luis Federico Leloir (Chemistry, 1970), César Milstein (1984) and Mario Molina (Chemistry, 1995).
