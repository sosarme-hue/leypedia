On 17 December 1903, at half past ten in the morning, on windswept dunes in North Carolina, a machine of wood and fabric took off with a man on board and flew for twelve seconds. It was the first controlled flight of a powered aeroplane, and it had been built by two brothers who ran a bicycle shop.

{fig:img/d/Q197.webp|d/airplane.html|The first flight of the Wright Flyer, 1903. Image: Wikimedia Commons.}

Two brothers from Dayton

{p:wilbur-wright|Wilbur} and {p:orville-wright|Orville Wright} lived in Dayton, Ohio. They had no university degree, but great mechanical skill, which they honed in their bicycle workshop. In 1896 they heard of the death of Otto Lilienthal, a German gliding pioneer, and decided to study the problem.

The secret was control

The Wrights understood that the great challenge was not the engine but control: how to keep balance and turn in the air. By watching birds they devised a way of twisting the wingtips. Between 1900 and 1902 they tested gliders at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, where the wind was steady and the sand softened falls. They also built their own wind tunnel to measure hundreds of wing shapes.

Twelve seconds

The Wright Flyer had a petrol engine they built themselves with their mechanic, Charlie Taylor, because no commercial engine was light enough. They tossed a coin to decide who would fly first; Wilbur won, but his attempt on 14 December failed. On the 17th it was Orville's turn: he flew about 37 metres in 12 seconds. They made four flights that day, and the last, piloted by Wilbur, covered 260 metres in 59 seconds. A man from the nearby lifesaving station took the famous photograph of the take-off.

The world was slow to believe it

Few newspapers reported it. The Wrights kept improving their {d:airplane|aeroplane} in Ohio, and only in 1908, with demonstrations in France and the United States, did the whole world accept that humans had learned to fly.

From the Flyer to the Moon

Wilbur died in 1912 and Orville in 1948, when planes were already crossing oceans. The original Flyer is kept at the National Air and Space Museum in Washington. And in 1969 Neil Armstrong took a piece of fabric from its wings to the Moon on {d:apollo-11|Apollo 11}: just 66 years later.
