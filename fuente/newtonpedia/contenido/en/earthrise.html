On Christmas Eve 1968, three astronauts circling the Moon saw something no human had seen before: the Earth rising over the lunar horizon, blue and white, against the black of space. The photograph they took is called «Earthrise», and it is one of the most famous in history.

{fig:img/p/Q18762.webp|p/frank-borman.html|Frank Borman, commander of Apollo 8. Image: Wikimedia Commons.}

A risky mission

{d:apollo-8|Apollo 8} lifted off on 21 December 1968 with {p:frank-borman|Frank Borman}, {p:jim-lovell|Jim Lovell} and William Anders on board. They were to be the first humans to leave Earth orbit and reach the Moon. NASA had brought the mission forward so as not to fall behind the Soviet Union in the space race, and it was the first time the huge Saturn V rocket carried a crew.

The photo

On 24 December, on the fourth orbit of the Moon, the spacecraft turned and the Earth appeared in the windows. Anders first took a black-and-white picture and then, with a colour film, the image that would go around the world. All three later spoke of how small and fragile the Earth looked from there.

The Christmas Eve reading

That same night the astronauts made a television broadcast from lunar orbit, watched by hundreds of millions of people. They read the first verses of Genesis and ended by wishing a merry Christmas to everyone on the «good Earth».

An image that changed how we see

The photo was printed in magazines worldwide and appeared on a US stamp in 1969. Many regard it as the start of the modern environmental movement: the first Earth Day was held in 1970. Anders summed it up in a much-quoted idea: they had gone to explore the Moon, and what they discovered was the Earth.

Afterwards

Lovell went back to the Moon on the troubled {d:apollo-13|Apollo 13}. Seven months after Apollo 8, {d:apollo-11|Apollo 11} finally landed on the lunar surface. For more great adventures in flight, read {art:wright|the story of the Wright brothers}.
