On the night of 5 December, in many villages in Austria, Bavaria and South Tyrol, the streets fill with figures covered in fur, with horns, cowbells and terrifying masks. It is the night of {d:krampus|Krampus}, the dark companion of Saint Nicholas. In European folklore, December is not only a month of lights.

Saint Nicholas's punisher

Saint Nicholas, the generous bishop who inspired Santa Claus, is celebrated on 6 December. On the eve, in the Alpine tradition, the saint visits homes to reward children who have behaved well. Krampus comes with him to frighten the rest: he carries chains, a bundle of birch switches and sometimes a basket on his back, in which he threatens to carry off the naughtiest.

A very old figure

The name seems to come from «Krampen», an old German word for claw. Many scholars believe Krampus has roots in pagan winter festivals, in which young men dressed as wild beings to drive away evil spirits. The Church eventually absorbed him into the Christian feast as a tamed demon serving the saint. He is documented from the sixteenth century.

Postcards and parades

In the nineteenth century «Krampuskarten» became popular in Austria: Christmas postcards showing Krampus chasing children or adults. Today the parades, known as «Krampuslauf», draw thousands of people, and the figure has become famous worldwide thanks to the cinema.

Other fearsome winter beings

  • {d:perchta|Perchta}: an Alpine woman who visited homes during the twelve nights of Christmas. She rewarded those who had worked and harshly punished the lazy.
  • The {d:jolasveinar|Yule Lads}: in Iceland, thirteen mischievous trolls come down from the mountains in the days before Christmas. They are the sons of Grýla, an ogress who, according to legend, eats disobedient children.
  • The Yule Cat: also Icelandic, it eats anyone who has not received new clothes before the holidays. The tradition encouraged people to finish their wool work on time.
  • Knecht Ruprecht: in Germany, another companion of Saint Nicholas, dressed in brown and carrying a rod.

Why so many monsters in December?

The longest nights of the year have always been filled with fears. These figures helped to bring up children, but they also gave shape to the darkness of winter and, by defeating it with feasting and noise, looked forward to the return of the light.
