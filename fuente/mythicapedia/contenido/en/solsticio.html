Around 21 December the Sun rises later and sets earlier than on any other day of the year in the northern hemisphere. It is the winter solstice. For ancient peoples it was a moment of fear and hope: the Sun seemed to be dying, but from then on it began to be reborn. No wonder so many myths and festivals gather around these dates.

Newgrange: a tomb that waits for the Sun

In Ireland there is a great stone mound built more than 5,000 years ago, before the pyramids of Egypt. Above its entrance is a small opening. Only for a few days around the winter solstice, at sunrise, does a ray of sunlight enter through it and travel down the passage to light up the chamber at the back for a few minutes.

Yule and the Wild Hunt

The Germanic and Norse peoples held a midwinter festival called Yule, «jól» in Old Norse, with feasts, ale and sacrifices. According to folklore, on those dark nights the Wild Hunt rode across the sky, a troop of mounted spirits often led by {d:odin|Odin}. Some northern European Christmas customs, such as the Yule log, are linked to those festivals.

Sol Invictus

In Rome, the emperor Aurelian dedicated a great temple to {d:sol-invictus|Sol Invictus}, the unconquered Sun, in the year 274. Its festival, the «birthday of the Unconquered Sun», was held on 25 December, when it was already clear that the days were growing longer again.

Mithras, the soldiers' god

Closely linked to the Sun was {d:mitra|Mithras}, a god of Persian origin whose cult spread among Roman soldiers. His followers met in underground temples built to look like a cave, which always showed the same scene: Mithras killing a bull. It was a mystery religion, with secret initiations, and some scholars have also placed the birth of Mithras at the solstice.

More suns reborn

In Egypt, the god {d:ra|Ra} crossed the underworld every night and was reborn every morning. The Greeks imagined {d:helios|Helios} driving his chariot of fire across the sky. In almost every culture, the return of the light after the longest night has been a reason to celebrate. In a way, our Christmas lights carry on the same tradition.
