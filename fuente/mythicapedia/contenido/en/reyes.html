Every year on the night of 5 January, children in Spain and many other countries wait for Melchior, Caspar and Balthazar. But who were the Three Wise Men? The account that tells their story is very short, and almost everything we know about them was added later by tradition. Their story is a good example of how a legend is born.

What the Gospel says

Only the Gospel of Matthew mentions them, and in just a few lines. It says that «Magi from the East» came to Jerusalem following a star, asked about the newborn king of the Jews, found the child in Bethlehem and offered him gold, frankincense and myrrh. The text does not say they were kings, does not say there were three and does not give their names.

What was a magus?

The word «magus» came from Persian and referred to a caste of priests of the religion of Zoroaster, famous for studying the stars and interpreting dreams. In ancient Persia one of the gods worshipped was {d:mitra|Mithra}. For readers of the time, magi following a star made perfect sense.

Three, kings and named

The number three was inferred from the three gifts. The idea that they were kings drew on a psalm that speaks of kings bringing tribute. The names Melchior, Caspar and Balthazar appear around the sixth century, and in a famous mosaic in Ravenna they are written above three figures wearing Eastern caps. Later they were given different ages and linked to the three known continents: Europe, Asia and Africa.

The meaning of the gifts

Tradition explained the gifts like this: gold, for a king; frankincense, for a god; and myrrh, a resin used for embalming, for a man who would one day die.

Relics and parades

Since 1164 Cologne Cathedral in Germany has kept, in a golden shrine, remains attributed to the three Magi, which became a major place of pilgrimage. In Spain, the tradition that they bring presents on 6 January is very old, and the parades, called «cabalgatas», have been held since the late nineteenth century. The one in Alcoy claims to be the oldest.

And the star

People have tried to explain the star as a comet, a conjunction of planets or a supernova. Giotto painted it as a comet, perhaps inspired by the passage of Halley's Comet in 1301. Whatever it was, the guiding star still shines over every nativity scene.
