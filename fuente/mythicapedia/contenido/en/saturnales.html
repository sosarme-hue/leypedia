For a few days in December, Rome turned upside down. Schools and law courts closed, people exchanged gifts, slaves sat down at the table and their masters served them. This was the Saturnalia, the merriest festival of the Roman calendar.

Saturn's festival

It was held in honour of {d:saturno|Saturn}, the god of sowing and of time, whom the Romans identified with the Greek Titan {d:crono|Cronus}. It began on 17 December with a sacrifice at his temple in the Forum. The god's statue, whose feet were bound with woollen bands for the rest of the year, was untied for the festival. At first it lasted a single day, but the people stretched it to five days and then seven.

The golden age

According to legend, when {d:jupiter|Jupiter} drove him out of heaven, Saturn took refuge in Latium and ruled there over a golden age: there were no slaves and no private property, and the earth bore fruit without being tilled. The Saturnalia recalled that happy time when everyone was equal.

A world turned upside down

During the festival the rules were suspended. Citizens put aside the toga and wore casual clothes and the pileus, the cap of freed slaves. Gambling, normally forbidden, was allowed. In each household a «king of the festival» was chosen by lot and gave absurd orders to everyone else. The greeting was «Io Saturnalia!».

Candles and figurines

Typical gifts were wax candles, a symbol of the light that returns after the solstice, and small clay or pottery figures called «sigillaria». The poet Martial wrote many epigrams to go with these gifts. The philosopher Seneca complained that in December the whole city was sweating with festivity.

The origin of Christmas?

It is often said that the Christian Christmas copied the Saturnalia. The link is not so direct: the date of 25 December has other explanations. But it is true that some customs, such as the banquets, the gifts and the festive mood at the end of the year, look very much like those of a Rome that, for a few days, played at being equal.
