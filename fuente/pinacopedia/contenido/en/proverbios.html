How many proverbs fit in one painting? In the case of {o:los-proverbios-flamencos-viejo|Netherlandish Proverbs}, painted by {b:pieter-brueghel-el-viejo|Pieter Bruegel the Elder} in 1559, more than a hundred. The picture, now in the {m:gemaldegalerie-de-berlin|Gemäldegalerie in Berlin}, shows a Flemish village where every villager is literally doing what a popular saying describes. It is one big guessing game.

{fig:img/m/Q533619.jpg|o/los-proverbios-flamencos-viejo.html|Pieter Bruegel the Elder, Netherlandish Proverbs (1559). Gemäldegalerie, Berlin.}

A world upside down

The original title was something like The Topsy-Turvy World, and one detail says so: the globe with its cross pointing downwards hanging over a house door. The whole picture is about human folly: people who waste time, squander, deceive or do pointless things. Many of these sayings are still alive today, in Dutch and in other languages.

Twelve proverbs to look for

Banging your head against a brick wall. A man in armour charges a wall: insisting on the impossible.

Belling the cat. Another soldier tries to hang a bell round a cat's neck: daring what nobody else will.

Casting roses before swine. A man throws flowers to pigs: giving valuable things to those who cannot appreciate them, like our "pearls before swine".

Big fish eat little fish. It appears, literally, by the river.

Filling the well after the calf has drowned. Acting when it is too late.

Putting a blue cloak on someone. A woman covers her husband with a blue cloak: she is unfaithful to him.

Throwing money into the water. A man tosses coins into the river: wasting money.

Sitting on hot coals. Someone sits on burning embers: being impatient or nervous.

Carrying the daylight out in baskets. A man carries light in a basket: wasting time.

Pissing at the moon. Wasting effort on something futile.

One shears sheep, the other shears pigs. One gets wool and the other nothing: the same work brings gain to some and loss to others.

The blind leading the blind. In the background, a line of blind men walks towards the water; Bruegel returned to the theme in {o:la-parabola-de-los-ciegos-viejo|The Blind Leading the Blind}.

A painter who observed

Bruegel knew village life and popular culture well, and liked to portray it with humour and a touch of criticism. He did something similar with children's games, in {o:juego-de-ninos-viejo|Children's Games}, and with festivities in {o:la-boda-campesina-viejo|The Peasant Wedding}. If you go to Berlin, take a list of proverbs and try to find them: it is the best way to see the painting.
