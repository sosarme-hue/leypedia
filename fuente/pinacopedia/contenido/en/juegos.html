Christmas holidays, children at home, games in the street. In 1560 {b:pieter-brueghel-el-viejo|Pieter Bruegel the Elder} painted a picture devoted entirely to them: {o:juego-de-ninos-viejo|Children's Games}, now in the {m:museo-de-historia-del-arte-de-viena|Kunsthistorisches Museum in Vienna}. In a village square more than two hundred children play some eighty different games. It is almost an encyclopedia of play in the Renaissance.

{fig:img/m/Q697332.jpg|o/juego-de-ninos-viejo.html|Pieter Bruegel the Elder, Children's Games (1560). Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna.}

Games we still know

Many will sound familiar. There are children playing blind man's buff, leapfrog, knucklebones, spinning tops, hoops, stilts and handstands. Others play marbles, dolls or swings, and a few blow soap bubbles through a pipe. One group holds a pretend wedding, another a procession, and a boy rides a hobby horse.

Games we have lost

Other games are no longer played, such as blowing up a pig's bladder to make a ball. Historians have identified many of them by comparing the painting with texts of the time, and some are still debated.

Only children?

One detail is unsettling: many of these children have adult faces, serious, almost old. That is why some historians think the painting is also an allegory: adults behaving like children, busy with games of no importance. Like {art:proverbios|Netherlandish Proverbs}, it would be a portrait of the "world upside down".

A game to play at home

Open the picture full screen and search with the children: who is playing blind man's buff? where is the boy on stilts? how many spinning tops are there? It is a great way to look at a painting carefully, and to discover that five centuries ago children played almost the same games as now.

If you enjoy it, Bruegel has other crowded pictures, such as {o:los-cazadores-en-la-nieve-viejo|The Hunters in the Snow}, with its skaters, or {o:el-combate-entre-don-carnaval-y-dona-cuaresma-viejo|The Fight Between Carnival and Lent}.
