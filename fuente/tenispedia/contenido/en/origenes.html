The tennis we watch today is just over 150 years old, but its roots go back much further. This is the story of how a ball game from French monasteries ended up on the grass of Wimbledon.

{fig:img/r/Q725054.webp|t/walter-clopton-wingfield.html|Walter Clopton Wingfield. Image: unknown author (public domain), Wikimedia Commons.}

Jeu de paume

In medieval France people played jeu de paume, the «palm game»: players hit a ball with their hand over a rope. Later came gloves and, in the 16th century, rackets. It was played in enclosed halls with walls and sloping roofs, and became very popular with kings and nobles. In English it is now called real tennis, and it is still played on a handful of courts.

Why «tennis»?

The most accepted explanation is that it comes from the French tenez, «take this», which the server called out. The origin of the scoring, 15, 30, 40, is a mystery. Some link it to the hands of a clock and others to medieval betting, but nobody knows for sure.

Major Wingfield's invention

In the mid-19th century, vulcanised rubber balls made it possible to play outdoors, on grass. In 1874 the British officer {t:walter-clopton-wingfield|Walter Clopton Wingfield} patented a game with rackets, a net and an hourglass-shaped court, which he gave a Greek name, sphairistike. He sold boxes with everything needed, and the game spread very quickly through Britain and its colonies. It soon became known as lawn tennis.

Wimbledon, 1877

In 1877 the All England Croquet Club in Wimbledon held a tennis championship to pay for a new roller for its lawns. Rules very close to today's were set: a rectangular court, scoring in games and sets, two serves. Twenty-two players entered and {t:spencer-gore|Spencer Gore} won, in front of about 200 spectators. So the oldest tournament in the world was born.

The first majors

The US championship followed in 1881, the French in 1891 and the Australasian in 1905. Women began playing at Wimbledon in 1884, with victory for {t:maud-watson|Maud Watson}.
