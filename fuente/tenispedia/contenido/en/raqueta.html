If a player from 1975 watched a match today, perhaps what would surprise them most is the rackets. Bigger, lighter and far more powerful, they have changed the way the game is played. Here is their story, from wood to graphite.

{fig:img/r/Q16474.webp|t/john-mcenroe.html|John McEnroe, one of the last champions of the wooden era. Photo: Charlie Cowins (CC BY 2.0), Wikimedia Commons.}

A century of wood

From the beginnings of {art:origenes|modern tennis} until the 1970s, rackets were made of wood, with a small head of about 420 square centimetres and natural gut strings. They were heavy and unforgiving: you had to hit the ball right in the middle. {t:bill-tilden|Bill Tilden}, {t:rod-laver|Rod Laver} and {t:bjorn-borg|Björn Borg} won with them.

Steel and aluminium

In 1967 a steel racket appeared that became famous in the hands of {t:jimmy-connors|Jimmy Connors}, who used it for years. Aluminium followed. In 1976 the engineer Howard Head, who had already revolutionised skis, launched an aluminium racket with a much larger head that forgave off-centre hits.

The graphite revolution

In the early 1980s composite materials arrived, above all carbon fibre or graphite. They were light and stiff and allowed large heads without losing control. Within a few years wood vanished from the tour. {t:john-mcenroe|John McEnroe}, who had started with wood, switched to graphite, and the generation of {t:ivan-lendl|Lendl} and {t:steffi-graf|Steffi Graf} grew up with it. The game became faster and more powerful.

Strings matter too

For decades strings were made from natural gut, from cow or sheep intestine. Then came nylon and, in the 1990s, polyester, which lets players hit with much more topspin without the ball flying long. Many coaches believe polyester changed tennis more than graphite did.

The rules

In the 1990s the International Tennis Federation set a maximum racket size: 73.7 centimetres long and 31.7 wide. There is no weight limit, and each player customises their racket with lead tape and grips.
