On 20 September 1973, at the Houston Astrodome, a 29-year-old champion faced a 55-year-old former champion in a match watched by some 90 million people. It was called the «Battle of the Sexes», and it was much more than a tennis match.

{fig:img/a/partido-battle-of-the-sexes-king-v-riggs-1973.webp|p/battle-of-the-sexes-king-v-riggs-1973.html|The Battle of the Sexes, 1973. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.}

The protagonists

{t:bobby-riggs|Bobby Riggs} had won Wimbledon in 1939, but by 1973 he was above all a showman and a gambler. He claimed women's tennis was far inferior and that, even at his age, he could beat any woman. {t:billie-jean-king|Billie Jean King} was world number one and the leading campaigner for equal prize money for men and women.

«Mother's Day»

King at first turned the challenge down. Riggs then played the Australian {t:margaret-court|Margaret Court} in May 1973 and beat her easily, in a match the press called the «Mother's Day Massacre». King realised she had to accept.

The match

The show was huge: King entered the stadium on a Cleopatra-style litter carried by college athletes, and Riggs in a rickshaw pulled by models. But on court King was all business. She played from the baseline, made her opponent run and won 6-4, 6-3, 6-3. We tell it in {p:battle-of-the-sexes-king-v-riggs-1973|the match page}.

More than a match

That same year King drove the creation of the WTA, the players' association, and the US Open became the first major to pay men and women equally. The win in Houston gave that fight visibility, at the height of the women's movement. Wimbledon did not equalise prize money until 2007.

Afterwards

King won 39 Grand Slam titles across singles, doubles and mixed. She and Riggs ended up friends. The story reached the cinema in 2017 with Battle of the Sexes, and the women's team competition is now called the {k:billie-jean-king-cup|Billie Jean King Cup}.
