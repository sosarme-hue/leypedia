In Spain there is no Christmas without turrón. Millions of kilos are eaten every year, and the bars appear in supermarkets almost before summer is over. But this almond and honey sweet has a far longer history than our family dinners: it goes back to Antiquity, passed through the kitchens of al-Andalus and found its home in a small town near Alicante.

A sweet of honey and almonds

The basic recipe for {p:turron|turrón} is very simple: honey or sugar, toasted almonds and egg white, which gives the mixture body. Similar sweets already existed in Greece and Rome, where crushed nuts were mixed with honey. In the medieval Arab world very similar almond pastes were made, and most specialists believe turrón as we know it reached the Iberian Peninsula through al-Andalus. The name is more disputed: some link it to an Arabic word, others to the Latin torrere, "to toast".

Jijona and Alicante, the birthplace

The first known written recipe appears in the Manual de mujeres, an anonymous recipe book from the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. From the sixteenth century on, documents from Alicante and Jijona mention turrón frequently. In 1582 Alicante's town council paid part of its Christmas wages in turrón, and in 1595 Philip II tried to limit spending on these sweets in the city, a sign that they were already a real Christmas luxury.

Jijona, in the hills of Alicante, became the great centre of production. Beehives and almond trees lived side by side there, and every winter the turrón makers travelled all over Spain to sell their bars. Today there are two protected designations: Turrón de Jijona and Turrón de Alicante.

Hard and soft: the big difference

Alicante turrón is the hard one: whole almonds bound by the honey and egg-white mixture, covered with a thin wafer. Jijona turrón is the soft one: the almonds are ground into a smooth, oily paste. The ingredients are almost the same; what changes is how the almonds are treated. That is why many households buy one of each, and nobody ever agrees on which is better.

Turrón all over Spain

Besides the Alicante varieties, there is a turrón tradition in Agramunt (Lleida), with a hazelnut turrón shaped like a round cake; in Castuera (Badajoz), with its almond and honey turrón; in Casinos (Valencia) and in Cherta (Tarragona). Over time the name spread to other bars with no almonds or honey at all: chocolate, coconut, toasted egg yolk or fruit turrón. {p:mazapan|Marzipan}, another Christmas classic, is a close relative, although it is made from ground almonds and sugar without honey.

Turrón around the world

In Italy, torrone is also a winter sweet, with major centres of production in Cremona and Benevento. In France, Montélimar nougat adds pistachios and lavender honey. In Latin America there are aniseed turrones in Peru, peanut ones in several countries and turrón de doña Pepa, an October tradition in Lima. In the Philippines, on the other hand, "turon" is a fried banana roll. The word travelled as far as the sweet.

Trivia for after dinner

A record-breaking turrón. In December 2025, Santiago del Teide in Tenerife set the Guinness record for the longest turrón, at more than 1,151 metres.

Swiss inspiration. The creators of Toblerone are said to have been inspired by Italian torrone when they named their honey and almond chocolate.

Best at room temperature. Soft turrón tastes better if you take it out of the fridge a while before serving, and hard turrón cuts better with a warm knife.

What to serve with it

Turrón goes well with a sweet wine: a Moscatel or a Pedro Ximénez are the classic partners. If you prefer something without alcohol, try an espresso or a herbal tea.
