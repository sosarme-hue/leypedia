What Milan calls panettone, Buenos Aires calls pan dulce and Lima simply panetón. This tall, fragrant bread has crossed the ocean and become the great Christmas sweet of much of South America.

Milan's «big bread»

The name {p:panettone|panettone} comes from Milanese dialect: panetton, «big bread». There are charming legends about its origin, such as the kitchen boy Toni who saved a banquet at the Sforza court with an improvised loaf, but they have no documentary basis. The oldest record dates from 1599: an expense register of the Borromeo college in Pavia lists butter, raisins and spices to make Christmas breads for the students.

An industrial sweet

Modern panettone is a child of the 20th century. In 1919 Angelo Motta began making it on a large scale in Milan and gave it its tall shape, wrapped in paper. Soon after, Gioacchino Alemagna launched his own, and the rivalry between the two brands took it all over Italy. Sourdough and a slow rise of up to three days are the secret of its light crumb. After baking, it is hung upside down so it does not collapse.

The journey to South America

Italian emigrants took panettone to Argentina, Uruguay, Brazil, Peru and Venezuela between the late 19th and the mid-20th century. In Argentina and Uruguay it lives alongside Genoese pan dulce, lower and fruitier, made by neighbourhood bakeries. In Brazil it is panetone, and there is even a chocolate version, chocotone.

Peru, panetón champion

Peru is one of the world's biggest consumers of panetón per person. It is in every company Christmas hamper, eaten with hot chocolate at midnight on the 24th, and it also appears at the national holidays in July, when the traditional «chocolatada» is served.

How to serve it

In Italy it is cut in vertical wedges, like a cake. It can be eaten plain, with a sweet wine such as moscato, or toasted the next day with butter. Leftovers make an excellent bread pudding or French toast.
