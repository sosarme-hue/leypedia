At midnight on 31 December, millions of people in Spain eat twelve grapes to the rhythm of the clock's chimes, one for each month of the new year. It is one of the world's most curious traditions. Where does it come from?

{fig:img/p/Q10978.webp|p/uva.html|Grapes. Photo: Fir0002 (GFDL 1.2), Wikimedia Commons.}

Two explanations

The most repeated story says that in 1909 the grape growers of Alicante had a huge harvest and decided to sell the surplus as «lucky grapes». But there are earlier reports. In the 1880s, some Madrid newspapers already mentioned well-off families who, copying the French custom of champagne and grapes on New Year's Eve, ate grapes at midnight. Apparently a group of Madrid locals decided to do it in the Puerta del Sol, partly to mock the fashion. Over time both stories merged and the custom spread across the country.

The Puerta del Sol clock

Since 1962 television has broadcast the chimes from the clock of the Real Casa de Correos, in Madrid's Puerta del Sol. First come the quarter chimes, then the twelve strokes, three seconds apart. Many people mistake the quarters for the strokes and start too early.

Which grape to choose

The most traditional are those from Vinalopó, in Alicante, which have a designation of origin: Aledo table grapes, grown in bags on the bunch to protect them and delay ripening until December. Today they are also sold peeled and seedless, in tins of twelve, to make the race easier.

Tips to avoid choking

Pick small grapes. Seedless if you can.

Peel them first. They are easier to chew quickly.

Don't start on the quarters. Wait for the first real stroke.

Chew, don't swallow them whole. Better to finish late than to choke; for small children, cut them up.

And in other countries

The custom has spread to several Latin American countries, such as Mexico, Peru and Venezuela. Elsewhere there are different rituals to bring luck: lentils in Italy; black-eyed peas in the southern United States; long soba noodles for a long life in Japan; a cake with a hidden coin in Greece.

Toast afterwards with something sparkling. And if you fancy the story of the {p:uva|grape}, one of the oldest fruits in Mediterranean cooking, its page is on Culinapedia. Happy New Year!
