Little eels, golden buns, colourful fruits, lucky pigs: marzipan takes a thousand shapes depending on the country. In Spain we link it to Christmas and to Toledo, but it is a sweet that has travelled across half of Europe and much of the Americas.

Almonds and sugar

{p:mazapan|Marzipan} is essentially ground almonds and sugar, sometimes with egg. The paste is kneaded, shaped and, in some cases, lightly baked to brown it. It is a relative of {p:turron|turrón}, but without honey and with a much softer texture.

Persian, Arab or from Toledo?

Many stories circulate about its origin. One says it came from Persia; another, that the Muslims brought it to the peninsula; another, that the nuns of the convent of San Clemente in Toledo invented it after the battle of Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212, when there was no wheat but plenty of almonds. None of them is proven. What we do know is that documents in Toledo mention it from 1512.

A mysterious name

The name has no certain explanation either. Some link it to the Latin martius panis, «March bread»; others to the Italian marzapane, and the Royal Spanish Academy points to an Arabic origin. In older English it was called marchpane, and Shakespeare mentions it in Romeo and Juliet.

Toledo marzipan

Today Mazapán de Toledo has a protected geographical indication: almonds must make up at least half of its weight. The most typical shapes are the eels, filled with egg yolk or candied fruit, and small pieces called «figuritas», eaten mostly at Christmas, although in Toledo you can find them all year round.

From Lübeck to Palermo

In northern Europe, marzipan was a speciality of the Hanseatic League cities, above all Lübeck and Tallinn, which are still famous for it. Königsberg made a marzipan with a browned top, sometimes with jam. In Sicily, in Palermo, marzipan fruits are shaped and painted to look real. And in Austria, Mozartkugeln are balls of marzipan and nougat coated in chocolate.

And in the Americas

In Mexico, peanut marzipan is very popular; in Guatemala it is made with pumpkin seeds; in Venezuela, with cashews; in Colombia, with powdered milk. Each country has made it its own with what it had to hand.

Other relatives

The same paste gives us pan de Cádiz, huesos de santo and Catalan panellets. To carry on with seasonal sweets, see {art:turron|the story of turrón}.
