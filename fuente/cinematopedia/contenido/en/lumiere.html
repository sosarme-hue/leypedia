On 28 December 1895, in the Salon Indien of the Grand Café on the Boulevard des Capucines in Paris, a small group of people paid one franc each to see something new: moving pictures projected on a screen. The usual figure given is 33 spectators. That evening is considered the birth of cinema as a public show.

{fig:img/p/Q1995323.webp|e/origenes.html|The Kingdom of the Fairies (1903), by Georges Méliès, who was at that first screening. Poster: TMDB.}

Two brothers from Lyon

Auguste and Louis Lumière were the sons of a photographer and industrialist from Lyon, whose photographic plate factory was one of the most important in Europe. Inspired by Thomas Edison's kinetoscope, which let only one person at a time watch a film through a viewer, they devised the cinématographe: a light machine that could film, develop and project.

The first programme

The show lasted about twenty minutes and consisted of ten films, each less than a minute long. Among them was Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory, shot at the gate of their own factory in Lyon, and The Sprinkler Sprinkled, a little joke considered the first fiction comedy in film. The famous Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat was shown soon afterwards, and gave rise to the legend, probably exaggerated, of spectators fleeing the room in fright.

An invention «without a future»

In the audience was Georges Méliès, an illusionist who wanted to buy a machine. According to the story, Antoine Lumière, the father, refused and told him that cinema was an invention without a future. Méliès built his own camera and became the great magician of film trickery, with films such as A Trip to the Moon and {p:el-reino-de-las-hadas-1903|The Kingdom of the Fairies}.

Cinema travels the world

The Lumières sent operators to every continent to film and to project. Within months the cinématographe had reached London, New York, Madrid and Bombay. The brothers themselves left cinema in the early twentieth century to work on colour photography, but their invention was already unstoppable.
