Few directors have tied their name to a genre like {n:alfred-hitchcock|Alfred Hitchcock}. Born in London in 1899, he started out in silent films designing title cards and ended up as the «Master of Suspense», a brand so recognisable that audiences went to see «a Hitchcock» rather than a film with this or that star.

The bomb under the table

Hitchcock explained the difference between surprise and suspense with a famous example. If two people are chatting at a table and a bomb suddenly goes off, the audience gets a few seconds of surprise. But if the viewer knows from the start that there is a bomb under the table, set to go off at one o'clock, every minute of small talk becomes unbearable. The key, he said, was to give the audience information, not to hide it.

From England to Hollywood

In England he became famous with films such as {p:39-escalones-1935|The 39 Steps} (1935). In 1939 the producer David O. Selznick brought him to the United States, and his first American film, {p:rebeca-1940|Rebecca} (1940), won the Oscar for Best Picture. Curiously, Hitchcock never won the Oscar for Best Director, although he was nominated five times.

The golden years

Between the late forties and the early sixties he made a run of masterpieces that is hard to match: {p:encadenados-1946|Notorious}, {p:extranos-en-un-tren-1951|Strangers on a Train}, {p:la-ventana-indiscreta-1954|Rear Window}, {p:vertigo-1958|Vertigo}, {p:psicosis-1960|Psycho} and {p:los-pajaros-1963|The Birds}. His obsessions run through them all: the wrong man, the elegant and mysterious blonde, voyeurism, the fear of heights.

With Psycho he broke every rule: he killed off the leading lady halfway through and asked cinemas not to let anyone in once the film had started. It was one of his biggest hits.

The man who appeared in his films

Hitchcock had a habit of showing up for a few seconds in almost all his films: boarding a bus, walking a dog, winding a clock. Audiences looked out for him so much that he started placing his cameos near the beginning, so that they would not be distracted from the story.

His influence is enormous. Film-makers such as François Truffaut, Brian De Palma and Steven Spielberg have acknowledged their debt to him, and in 2012 Vertigo was voted the greatest film ever made in the Sight and Sound critics' poll.
