Churros and chocolate: the story of a classic

24 November 2026 · 5 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

Where churros come from, since when they have been dipped in chocolate, and what they are called around Spain.

Few things are as Spanish as a cup of thick hot chocolate and a plate of freshly made churros, especially on a cold morning or on the way home after a night out. But where do churros come from? And since when have we dipped them in chocolate?

An origin full of legends

A churro is a dough of flour, water and salt that is scalded, piped through a star-shaped nozzle and fried in oil. Its origin is unclear. It is often said that Spanish shepherds invented it to replace fresh bread in the mountains, but that is unlikely: frying takes a lot of oil, which is hard to carry around. Another story claims the Portuguese brought it from China, where the youtiao, a fried dough stick, exists, but there is no solid evidence. Most likely it was born in the Iberian Peninsula from the fried doughs of medieval cooking, such as fritters.

The word

According to the Royal Spanish Academy, «churro» is onomatopoeic: it imitates the sound of the dough sizzling in the oil. Further proof that a churro is, above all, a street sound and smell.

Chocolate came first

Chocolate arrived from Mexico in the 16th century and quickly became popular in Spain, where it was drunk hot, thick and sweetened, long before the rest of Europe. Churros joined later: dipping them in chocolate became popular in the 19th century, with the chocolate shops and churro stalls of Madrid. Some, like San Ginés, opened in 1894, still serve churros at all hours.

Churros, porras, calentitos and tejeringos

Spain has its own churro map. In Madrid, thin crunchy churros live alongside porras, thicker and fluffier. In Seville they are called calentitos, and in Málaga and other parts of Andalusia, tejeringos, fried in a large spiral and cut with scissors. In some places they have no ridges and no sugar.

Around the world

In Portugal they are eaten at fairs and carnivals. In Latin America they are filled with dulce de leche, chocolate or guava, and in Mexico they are sold on the street dusted with sugar and cinnamon. In the Philippines they are a Christmas classic, and in the United States they are a hit at theme parks.

Tips for making them at home

Boiling water. The flour is scalded with boiling water and a pinch of salt; the dough should be smooth and fairly firm.

Star nozzle. The ridges are not just decoration: they help the churros fry evenly and stop the dough from bursting.

Very hot oil. About 180 °C, in small batches, then drain on paper.

And the chocolate should be thick, the kind that holds on a spoon. If you still have a sweet tooth, read the story of turrón.
