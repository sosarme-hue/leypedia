    The most extreme places on Earth

    19 November 2026 · 5 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

    The hottest, the coldest, the driest, the highest and the deepest: a journey through the planet’s records.

    Earth is a friendly planet for life, but it has corners where almost nothing could survive. Places so hot, cold, dry or high that they seem to belong to another world. Here are some of the planet's most astonishing records.

    The hottest place

    In Death Valley, California, the highest air temperature on record was measured in 1913: 56.7 °C, although some experts doubt that reading. If we look at heat all year round, one of the hottest places is Dallol, in the Danakil Depression in Ethiopia. There, hot springs form yellow, green and orange pools so acidic that almost no living thing can survive in them.

    The coldest place

    On 21 July 1983, at the Russian Vostok station in the heart of Antarctica, the thermometer dropped to −89.2 °C, the lowest air temperature ever measured at a weather station. Satellites have detected even lower surface temperatures in some hollows of the Antarctic plateau, close to −98 °C.

    The driest and the wettest

    The Atacama Desert in Chile is the driest non-polar desert in the world: in some areas no rain has been recorded for many years, and scientists use it to rehearse missions to Mars. At the other extreme, some villages in the Meghalaya region of India, such as Mawsynram, receive more than ten metres of monsoon rain a year. You will find more dry landscapes on our deserts page.

    The highest and the deepest

    The summit of Everest, at 8,849 metres, is the highest point above sea level. Up there the air holds a third of the oxygen we breathe at the coast. The deepest point is in the Mariana Trench, in the Pacific Ocean: the Challenger Deep, almost 11,000 metres below the surface. If we put Everest at the bottom, its summit would still be more than two kilometres under water.
