    Why is the sky blue?

    19 November 2026 · 5 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

    Air is transparent and sunlight is white. So where does the blue come from? And why are sunsets red?

    It is one of the questions children ask most, and the answer is not obvious: air is transparent, sunlight is white, and yet the sky on a clear day is blue. The explanation lies in how sunlight passes through the atmosphere.

    White light carries every colour

    Sunlight looks white, but it is a mixture of all the colours of the rainbow. Each colour is a wave of a different size: red has the longest wavelength, and blue and violet the shortest. We see this when light passes through a prism or through raindrops and splits into a rainbow.

    Air scatters blue the most

    When light enters the atmosphere, it bumps into the nitrogen and oxygen molecules in the air. These molecules are much smaller than light waves and deflect them in all directions, but not equally: they scatter short, blue waves far more than long, red ones. So wherever we look, blue light reaches us, bounced from all over the sky. The British physicist Lord Rayleigh explained this in the nineteenth century, and it bears his name: Rayleigh scattering.

    So why isn't it violet?

    Violet is scattered even more than blue, so you might expect a violet sky. It isn't, for three reasons: the Sun gives off less violet light than blue, some of it is absorbed high in the atmosphere, and our eyes are much less sensitive to violet. The result, to us, is blue.

    Red sunsets and skies on other worlds

    At sunrise and sunset, sunlight has to cross much more atmosphere to reach us. Along the way it loses almost all its blue, and what is left are the oranges and reds. Dust and moisture can make those colours even more intense.

    On other planets the opposite can happen. On Mars, the fine dust in its atmosphere tints the daytime sky a butterscotch colour, while its sunsets are bluish.
