    Althea Gibson, the pioneer

    1 December 2026 · 5 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

    The first Black player to win the French Championships, Wimbledon and the US Championships, in the 1950s.

    In 1950, in the United States, a Black player could not enter the major tennis tournaments. Seven years later Althea Gibson received the Wimbledon trophy from Queen Elizabeth II. Hers is the story of a pioneer who opened a door that seemed shut.

    From Harlem to the courts

    Gibson was born in 1927 in South Carolina, into a family of farmers, and grew up in Harlem, New York. She started playing paddle tennis in the street, and her talent caught the eye of clubs in the American Tennis Association, the organisation of Black players. For years she won its championships, but the official association's tournaments were closed to her.

    Alice Marble's letter

    In 1950 the former champion Alice Marble published a letter in a tennis magazine denouncing Gibson's exclusion. That same year she was finally allowed to play at Forest Hills, and in 1951 she became the first Black player at Wimbledon.

    The titles

    In 1956 she won Roland Garros, the first Grand Slam title by a Black player, and also the Wimbledon doubles with the British player Angela Buxton, who was Jewish and also faced discrimination. In 1957 and 1958 she won Wimbledon and the US championship. Her first Wimbledon final is told in this page. New York welcomed her with a ticker-tape parade.

    A hard life

    Amateur tennis paid nothing, and Gibson retired in 1958. She tried singing, acting and, later, became the first Black woman on the professional golf tour. She struggled for money in her final years, and her old partner Angela Buxton helped support her. She died in 2003.

    Her legacy

    Gibson paved the way for Arthur Ashe and, decades later, for Venus and Serena Williams, who always cited her as a role model. In 2019 the US Open unveiled a sculpture of her at Flushing Meadows.
