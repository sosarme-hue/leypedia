    Suzanne Lenglen and the match of the century

    19 November 2026 · 6 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

    Women's tennis's first great star and her only meeting with Helen Wills, in Cannes in 1926.

    Before her, women played in ankle-length skirts, corsets and hats. Suzanne Lenglen walked on court in a knee-length skirt, bare arms, a coloured bandeau in her hair and a game nobody had seen before. She was the first great star of women's tennis and played the so-called match of the century.

    La Divine

    Born in Paris in 1899, she learned the game from her father, who trained her with very demanding methods. In 1919, aged twenty, she won Wimbledon in a memorable final, and from then on she hardly ever lost. She won Wimbledon six times and the French championship six times, and took gold at the 1920 Antwerp Olympics. The French called her «la Divine». She sipped cognac between sets, leapt like a dancer and filled the stands wherever she played.

    The duel with Helen Wills

    In the mid-1920s a rival appeared: the American Helen Wills, seven years younger, serious and unflappable. Everyone wanted to see them play, but they did not meet at tournaments. In February 1926 Wills travelled to the Riviera, and on 16 February they met in the final of a tournament at the Carlton Club in Cannes.

    The excitement was huge: journalists from all over the world, tickets resold at sky-high prices, people climbing onto roofs and trees to catch a glimpse. Lenglen won 6-3, 8-6, but finished exhausted. It was the only time they ever played each other. We tell it in detail in the match page.

    Turning professional

    That same year at Wimbledon, a misunderstanding over the schedule of a match in front of Queen Mary ended with Lenglen withdrawing from the tournament. Soon afterwards she accepted a professional tour of the United States, something almost scandalous at the time, and left amateur tennis for good. She died in 1938, aged just 39.

    Her legacy

    Lenglen changed the image of women in sport and showed that women's tennis could fill stadiums. Today the second court at Roland Garros bears her name, and the women's champion's trophy there is the Coupe Suzanne Lenglen.
