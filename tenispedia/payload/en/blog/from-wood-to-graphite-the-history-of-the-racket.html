    From wood to graphite: the history of the racket

    22 December 2026 · 5 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

    How the player's tool changed, and the way the game is played changed with it.

    If a player from 1975 watched a match today, perhaps what would surprise them most is the rackets. Bigger, lighter and far more powerful, they have changed the way the game is played. Here is their story, from wood to graphite.

    A century of wood

    From the beginnings of modern tennis until the 1970s, rackets were made of wood, with a small head of about 420 square centimetres and natural gut strings. They were heavy and unforgiving: you had to hit the ball right in the middle. Bill Tilden, Rod Laver and Björn Borg won with them.

    Steel and aluminium

    In 1967 a steel racket appeared that became famous in the hands of Jimmy Connors, who used it for years. Aluminium followed. In 1976 the engineer Howard Head, who had already revolutionised skis, launched an aluminium racket with a much larger head that forgave off-centre hits.

    The graphite revolution

    In the early 1980s composite materials arrived, above all carbon fibre or graphite. They were light and stiff and allowed large heads without losing control. Within a few years wood vanished from the tour. John McEnroe, who had started with wood, switched to graphite, and the generation of Lendl and Steffi Graf grew up with it. The game became faster and more powerful.

    Strings matter too

    For decades strings were made from natural gut, from cow or sheep intestine. Then came nylon and, in the 1990s, polyester, which lets players hit with much more topspin without the ball flying long. Many coaches believe polyester changed tennis more than graphite did.

    The rules

    In the 1990s the International Tennis Federation set a maximum racket size: 73.7 centimetres long and 31.7 wide. There is no weight limit, and each player customises their racket with lead tape and grips.
