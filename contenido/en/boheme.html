It is Christmas Eve in a freezing garret in the Latin Quarter of Paris. Four penniless young artists burn a manuscript to keep warm, trick the landlord and go out to celebrate at the Café Momus. So begins La bohème by {b:giacomo-puccini|Giacomo Puccini}, one of the most performed operas in the world and, without meaning to be, one of the most Christmassy.

From a serialised novel to an opera

The story comes from Henri Murger's Scenes of Bohemian Life, sketches of the lives of poor artists in Paris that were published in instalments in the mid-nineteenth century. Puccini commissioned the libretto from two writers he already worked with, {b:luigi-illica|Luigi Illica} and {b:giuseppe-giacosa|Giuseppe Giacosa}. The collaboration was stormy: Puccini demanded endless changes, and Giacosa threatened several times to walk out.

To complicate matters, the composer {b:ruggero-leoncavallo|Ruggero Leoncavallo}, author of Pagliacci, was writing his own Bohème on the same novel at the same time. The two composers became embroiled in a public quarrel in the press. Puccini premiered first, and although Leoncavallo's version was successful in its day, it is rarely staged now.

Turin, 1 February 1896

The premiere took place at the Teatro Regio in Turin, conducted by a twenty-eight-year-old {b:arturo-toscanini|Arturo Toscanini}. The critics were cool: some thought it a minor opera, too simple and sentimental. The public, however, took to it at once, and within a few years it was being performed all over the world.

What happens on Christmas Eve

The first two acts take place on the night of 24 December. In the first, the poet Rodolfo is alone in the garret when his neighbour Mimì knocks at the door because her candle has gone out. She drops her key, they look for it in the dark and their hands meet. Then come three of the opera's most famous moments:

  • "Che gelida manina" ("What a cold little hand"): Rodolfo introduces himself and tells of his life as a poor but happy poet.
  • "Sì, mi chiamano Mimì": she answers with her own story, that of a seamstress who embroiders cloth flowers and longs for spring.
  • "O soave fanciulla": the love duet that ends the act, as the two leave to join their friends.

The second act is an explosion of Christmas life: the street full of vendors, children begging the toy-seller Parpignol, the military band and, at the Café Momus, the arrival of Musetta, Marcello's former girlfriend, who sings the waltz "Quando m'en vo'" to make him jealous. Twenty minutes of almost cinematic pace.

The third and fourth acts are no longer Christmassy: winter comes, Mimì falls ill, and the opera has one of the saddest endings in the repertoire. But if you are looking for something for the night of the 24th, the first two acts last just over an hour and are perfect.

Two legendary recordings

  • {b:thomas-beecham|Thomas Beecham} recorded a Bohème in 1956 with {b:victoria-de-los-angeles|Victoria de los Ángeles} and {b:jussi-bjorling|Jussi Björling} that many consider the most poetic in recording history. The Barcelona-born soprano is a Mimì of unforgettable tenderness.
  • {b:herbert-von-karajan|Karajan} recorded it in 1972 with {b:mirella-freni|Mirella Freni} and {b:luciano-pavarotti|Luciano Pavarotti}. Both were born in Modena in 1935, and it is said that, as their mothers worked together, they shared the same wet nurse as babies. Few couples have sung together with such rapport.
