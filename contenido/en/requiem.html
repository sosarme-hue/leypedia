On 5 December 1791, shortly before one in the morning, {b:wolfgang-amadeus-mozart|Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart} died in Vienna. He was thirty-five and left a mass for the dead unfinished, the Requiem. Few works have generated so many legends: a mysterious messenger, a poisoning rival, a composer writing his own funeral music. Let us separate what we know from what has been invented.

The mysterious commission

In the summer of 1791 a stranger came to Mozart's house to commission a requiem, on condition that he did not ask who wanted it. Today we know who was behind it: Count Franz von Walsegg, a music-loving aristocrat who had lost his wife in February that year. Walsegg was in the habit of commissioning works from composers and then presenting them as his own, and he intended to do the same with the mass in his wife's memory.

Mozart accepted, but he had many other commitments: that same year he finished The Magic Flute, La clemenza di Tito and the Clarinet Concerto. The Requiem progressed slowly.

What Mozart left on paper

When he died, only the first movement, the "Introitus", was complete. For the "Kyrie" and most of the "Sequence" and the "Offertory" he had written the choral parts, the bass line and some instructions for the orchestra. The "Lacrimosa", one of the most moving moments in the work, breaks off after its first eight bars. Nothing in his hand survives of the "Sanctus", the "Benedictus" or the "Agnus Dei".

Constanze and the race to finish it

His widow, Constanze, was left with debts and two small children, and she needed to deliver the complete Requiem to collect the rest of the fee. She first asked the composer Joseph Eybler, who began to orchestrate it but gave up. She then turned to {b:franz-xaver-sussmayr|Franz Xaver Süssmayr}, Mozart's pupil and assistant, who completed the orchestration, finished the "Lacrimosa" and wrote the "Sanctus", "Benedictus" and "Agnus Dei". For the ending, Süssmayr repeated the music of the opening, apparently as Mozart had indicated.

That is the version almost always performed. In the twentieth century several musicologists proposed other completions that try to come closer to Mozart's style, but Süssmayr's remains the best loved, perhaps because it is the one we all know.

The Salieri legend

A few years after Mozart's death a rumour began to circulate that his colleague {b:antonio-salieri|Antonio Salieri} had poisoned him. There is no evidence at all. The rumour inspired a short play by the Russian poet Pushkin in 1830, and in 1979 Peter Shaffer's play Amadeus, filmed in 1984, which turned Salieri into the most famous envious man in history. The reality is very different: Salieri was a respected musician and a great teacher, whose pupils included {b:ludwig-van-beethoven|Beethoven} and {b:franz-peter-schubert|Schubert}.

Nor is it true that Mozart believed he was writing his own requiem for a messenger from the beyond. That Romantic image grew out of Constanze's accounts and those of the first biographers, who embellished his last months considerably.

How to listen

Notice the opening: bassoons and basset horns, a kind of low clarinet, create a dark, veiled colour that Mozart chose deliberately. Listen to the "Dies irae", which bursts like a storm; the "Tuba mirum" with its solo trombone; the "Confutatis", where the men shout and the women answer as if praying from afar; and finally the "Lacrimosa". When you reach the eighth bar, remember that this is where Mozart's hand stopped.

For recordings, you have the intensity of {b:karl-bohm|Karl Böhm} with the Vienna Philharmonic, the solemnity of {b:herbert-von-karajan|Karajan}, or the lighter, more dramatic period-instrument readings of {b:nikolaus-harnoncourt|Nikolaus Harnoncourt} and {b:john-eliot-gardiner|John Eliot Gardiner}.
