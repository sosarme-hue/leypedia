We do not know for certain on which day {b:ludwig-van-beethoven|Ludwig van Beethoven} was born. We know he was baptised in Bonn on 17 December 1770 and, since children were usually baptised the day after birth, his birthday is generally celebrated on the 16th. To mark the anniversary, here are ten curiosities about the composer who changed the history of music.

1. A grandfather with the same name

His grandfather, also called Ludwig, was a Flemish musician who became Kapellmeister at the Bonn court. Beethoven barely knew him, as he died when the boy was three, but he kept a portrait of him all his life and spoke of him with pride.

2. A father who wanted another Mozart

His father, Johann, a court singer, saw his son's talent and wanted to turn him into a child prodigy like {b:wolfgang-amadeus-mozart|Mozart}. He made him practise for hours, sometimes at night, and at his first public concert the boy was announced as six years old when he was in fact seven. For a long time Beethoven himself believed he was younger than he was.

3. His first great teacher

In Bonn he studied with {b:christian-gottlob-neefe|Christian Gottlob Neefe}, the court organist, who had him study Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier and published his first works. In 1783 Neefe wrote in a music journal that, if he carried on like this, the boy would become "a second Mozart".

4. "Mozart's spirit from Haydn's hands"

In 1792 Beethoven moved to Vienna to study with {b:joseph-haydn|Joseph Haydn}. Before he left, his patron Count Waldstein wrote in a farewell album: "You will receive Mozart's spirit from Haydn's hands." The relationship with Haydn was not easy, and Beethoven also took lessons from other teachers, among them {b:antonio-salieri|Antonio Salieri}, with whom he studied vocal composition.

5. Deafness

In the late 1790s, before he was thirty, he began to lose his hearing. In 1802, in the village of Heiligenstadt, he wrote a letter to his brothers, never sent, confessing that he had thought of taking his own life and that only his art had held him back. This document, known as the "Heiligenstadt Testament", was found among his papers after his death.

6. The torn dedication to Napoleon

His pupil {b:ferdinand-ries|Ferdinand Ries} recalled that Beethoven had dedicated his Third Symphony to Napoleon Bonaparte and that, on learning that he had proclaimed himself emperor, he furiously tore up the title page. The symphony was finally published as the Eroica, "composed to celebrate the memory of a great man".

7. Teacher of teachers

One of his piano pupils was {b:carl-czerny|Carl Czerny}, famous today for the exercises every piano student knows. Czerny in turn taught Franz Liszt, so a direct line links Beethoven with the great Romantic virtuoso. You can follow these chains of teachers and pupils in the Teachers & pupils section of Classicapedia.

8. The conversation books

In his last years, completely deaf, Beethoven communicated through notebooks in which visitors wrote their questions and he answered aloud. About 140 survive, an extraordinary source on his daily life, although his secretary Anton Schindler destroyed or forged some pages.

9. The Ninth and the applause he could not hear

At the premiere of the Ninth Symphony in 1824, Beethoven stood on stage beside the conductor. When it ended, he remained with his back to the audience, unable to hear the ovation, until the contralto Caroline Unger turned him round to see the hall applauding and waving handkerchiefs and hats.

10. A crowded funeral

Beethoven died in Vienna on 26 March 1827. Thousands of people attended his funeral, a crowd never before seen for a musician. Among the torchbearers was {b:franz-peter-schubert|Franz Schubert}, who admired him deeply and who would die only some eighteen months later.

To celebrate

A suggestion for 16 December: the "Moonlight" Sonata in the morning, the Fifth Symphony after lunch and, in the evening, the last movement of the Ninth. And if you want more, his biography covers the rest of his work, from the string quartets to Fidelio.
