Every 22 November, bands, choirs and conservatoires around the world celebrate Saint Cecilia, the patron saint of music. In many Spanish towns it is the day of the local band, with parades, concerts and a shared meal. But why did a Roman martyr become the protector of musicians? And above all, what music has been written in her honour?

A patron saint born of a misunderstanding

According to tradition, Cecilia was a young Roman noblewoman martyred in the early centuries of Christianity. Her legend says that on her wedding day, while the instruments played, she "sang to God in her heart". The Latin antiphon for her feast said cantantibus organis, "while the instruments played". Over time organis was read as "organ", and painters began to portray her playing the organ, the lute or the viol. From there to patron saint of music was only a short step.

In the sixteenth century a congregation of musicians was founded in Rome under her name, the origin of today's Accademia Nazionale di Santa Cecilia, and her feast became an occasion for musicians all over Europe.

London, 1683: a tradition of odes is born

In 1683 a London musical society began to celebrate every 22 November with a concert and an ode written for the occasion. The first to set one was {b:henry-purcell|Henry Purcell}, with Welcome to all the pleasures. Ten years later he composed the most ambitious of them, Hail, bright Cecilia (1692), in which each instrument receives its praise: the recorder, the violin, the organ and the trumpets. It is one of the most brilliant works of the English Baroque and a fine way to start the day.

Handel and Dryden's poem

For the 1687 celebration the poet John Dryden wrote A Song for St Cecilia's Day, which half a century later was set to music by {b:george-frideric-handel|George Frideric Handel}. His Ode for St Cecilia's Day (1739) describes how music creates the universe out of chaos and how each instrument awakens a passion: the trumpet, courage; the flute, sorrow; the organ, devotion. If you enjoyed our {art:mesias|Messiah listening guide}, this ode is the natural next step.

Gounod: a mass for Saint Cecilia

In the nineteenth century the feast moved from London to Paris. {b:charles-francois-gounod|Charles Gounod}, composer of Faust, first performed his St Cecilia Mass in 1855 at the church of Saint-Eustache. It is a mass for large orchestra, with harps and a very operatic melody in the "Sanctus", and for decades it was one of the most performed sacred works in France.

Parry: the Victorian ode

{b:hubert-parry|Hubert Parry}, one of the key figures of the English musical renaissance, turned to Pope's poem and wrote his own Ode on St Cecilia's Day (1889). It is music for choir and orchestra in the great Victorian tradition, less well known than Purcell's and Handel's but very rewarding.

Britten: the composer born on Saint Cecilia's Day

The loveliest story is that of {b:benjamin-britten|Benjamin Britten}, who was born on 22 November 1913. Britten always felt close to his patron saint, and in 1942 he wrote the Hymn to St Cecilia for unaccompanied choir, to a poem by his friend W. H. Auden. He finished it during the same sea voyage on which he composed A Ceremony of Carols, one of the works we recommend in {art:navidad|our list of Christmas music}. Its refrain, "Blessed Cecilia, appear in visions to all musicians", is almost a prayer for musicians of every age.

How to celebrate

A suggestion for 22 November: start the morning with Purcell's Hail, bright Cecilia, continue with Handel's ode and end the afternoon with Britten's hymn. Three centuries of English music dedicated to the same saint. And if your town has a band, go and hear it: band musicians are the ones who keep this feast most alive.
