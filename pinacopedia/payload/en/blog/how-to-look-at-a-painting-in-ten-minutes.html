How to look at a painting in ten minutes

19 November 2026 · 6 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

A simple method for enjoying any painting in a museum, step by step, with Las Meninas as an example.

We walk into a museum, go through twenty rooms and leave feeling we have not seen anything. That is perfectly normal: the brain tires quickly, and paintings ask for time. The good news is that you do not need to know art history to enjoy a painting. You just need to look slowly and with a method. Here is a ten-minute exercise, with a superb example: Velázquez's Las Meninas.

Minute 1: first impression

Before reading the label, stand still a few metres from the picture and ask yourself what you feel. Is it a cheerful scene, a solemn one, an unsettling one? Where does your eye go first? In Las Meninas almost everyone looks first at the Infanta Margarita, the fair-haired girl in the centre, because she is the most brightly lit figure.

Minutes 2 and 3: what you see

Describe quietly what is there, as if you were telling someone on the phone. People, objects, place, time of day. In Las Meninas there are eleven figures and a dog: the Infanta, her two maids of honour (the meninas), two court dwarfs, a lady-in-waiting and a guard in the half-light, a man in the doorway at the back, Velázquez himself painting a huge canvas and, in the mirror at the back, King Philip IV and Queen Mariana.

Minutes 4 and 5: light and composition

Notice where the light comes from and what it leaves in shadow. Look for the lines that organise the picture: diagonals, verticals, triangles. Velázquez lights the Infanta's group from the windows on the right and leaves the upper half of the room almost dark, with paintings barely visible on the walls. The open door at the back, with its strong light, gives the eye a second way out.

Minutes 6 and 7: move closer

Now get as close as the museum allows. Great painters work at two distances. From afar, the Infanta's dress looks like shining silk; up close, it is just a few loose brushstrokes, almost abstract. This sketchy way of painting fascinated the Impressionists two centuries later. Manet went as far as calling Velázquez "the painter of painters".

Minute 8: the questions

Every good picture hides a question. In Las Meninas, the big one is what Velázquez is painting. The portrait of the king and queen we see reflected in the mirror? Then the royal couple would be standing where we are, watching the scene. The viewer becomes part of the picture, which is why philosophers and artists have never stopped writing about it.

Minute 9: the context

Now is the time to read the label or the entry. Date, technique, who commissioned it. Knowing that Velázquez painted himself next to the royal family, wearing the red cross of the Order of Santiago that was actually added later, changes the reading: the picture also speaks of the ambition of a painter who wanted to be recognised as a nobleman.

Minute 10: take something with you

Choose one detail to remember: the drowsy dog, the dwarf's foot nudging it, the blurred reflection of the king and queen. If you like, take a photo of that detail (no flash) or make a quick sketch. The next day you will remember it far better than twenty pictures seen in passing.

Three tips for your visit

Choose a few works. It is better to see five paintings in depth than fifty in a rush. Look at the map first and decide what you want to see.

Go early or late. At the Prado or the Louvre, the most famous rooms fill up by mid-morning.

Try a difficult picture. Apply the ten minutes to a work packed with detail, such as Bruegel's Netherlandish Proverbs, with over a hundred sayings hidden in it. You will see that the method works with any painting.
