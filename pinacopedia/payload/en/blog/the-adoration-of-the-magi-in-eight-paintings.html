The Adoration of the Magi in eight paintings

19 November 2026 · 7 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

From Giotto to Velázquez: how the great masters painted the arrival of the Magi, and the secrets hidden in each version.

Few subjects have given painters as much to work with as the arrival of the Magi in Bethlehem. The Gospel of Matthew gives them only a few lines: wise men from the East follow a star, find the Child and offer him gold, frankincense and myrrh. It never says they were kings, or that there were three of them. Everything else (the names Melchior, Caspar and Balthasar, the crowns, the camels, the Black king) was added by tradition and, above all, by painting.

For an artist, the Adoration of the Magi was the perfect commission. It meant painting luxurious clothes, animals, processions and distant landscapes, and it also allowed the patron who paid for the picture to appear in it, dressed as a king or lost among the retinue. Here are eight versions that show how European painting changed over three centuries.

1. Giotto (around 1305): the star with a tail

In the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua, Giotto painted a simple, deeply human scene: the oldest king kneels and kisses the Child's feet, while a camel peers in with curiosity. Above the stable shines a star with a long tail of fire. Giotto is thought to have been inspired by Halley's Comet, which passed in 1301, and that is why the European probe that visited the comet in 1986 was named Giotto.

2. Gentile da Fabriano (1423): International Gothic luxury

The altarpiece Gentile painted for the banker Palla Strozzi, now in the Uffizi, is an explosion of gold, brocade and exotic animals: monkeys, cheetahs, falcons. The procession winds from the far landscape down to the foreground. It is the Adoration as a parade of Florentine wealth, and one of the last great Gothic pictures before the Renaissance.

3. Sandro Botticelli (around 1475): the Medici in Bethlehem

Here the Magi wear the faces of the Medici. The old man touching the Child's feet is Cosimo the Elder, and among the companions are his sons and his grandsons Lorenzo the Magnificent and Giuliano. Botticelli painted himself on the right-hand edge, wrapped in a yellow cloak and looking straight at us. The picture was really a declaration of loyalty from the patron to the most powerful family in the city.

4. Leonardo da Vinci (1481): the unfinished picture

Leonardo was commissioned by the monks of San Donato a Scopeto and left the work half done when he moved to Milan. What remains is an underdrawing on the panel, with a crowd of figures swirling around the Virgin, rearing horses and ruins in the background. Precisely because it is unfinished, it lets us see how Leonardo built his compositions. It is now in the Uffizi.

5. Albrecht Dürer (1504): the journey to Italy

Dürer painted this Adoration for Frederick the Wise of Saxony. You can see what he had learned in Italy: rigorous perspective with ruined arches, luminous colours and solid figures. The king with long curly hair has often been read as a self-portrait, and he closely resembles the portraits Dürer made of himself in those years.

6. Hieronymus Bosch (around 1494): the Magi and the Antichrist

Bosch's triptych in the Prado looks like a traditional Adoration, until you notice the half-naked figure peering out of the stable door, wearing a crown of thorns and with a wound on his leg. Many historians identify him as the Antichrist. Even at Christmas, Bosch reminded viewers that evil is always lying in wait.

7. Pieter Bruegel the Elder (1563): the Magi in the snow

Bruegel painted two very different versions in the same year. In the one at the National Gallery in London, the Adoration of the Kings, the Magi are almost caricatures and the soldiers eye the scene with suspicion. In the other, the Adoration of the Magi in the Snow, the event almost disappears: what we see is a Flemish village under a snowfall, with people carrying water and children playing on the ice. It is one of the first pictures in history in which snow actually falls.

8. Diego Velázquez (1619): a family from Seville

Velázquez was about twenty when he painted this Adoration, now in the Prado. Light falls from the side on faces that seem taken from the street, and tradition holds that the Virgin is his wife, Juana Pacheco, and the Child his newborn daughter, Francisca. Melchior may be his father-in-law and teacher, Francisco Pacheco, and the young king the painter himself. A very Sevillian, very intimate Christmas.

To see more

If you get the chance, the Prado Museum gathers the Adorations by Bosch, Velázquez and Rubens within a few rooms: a good excuse for a visit at this time of year. And if you like seeing snow enter painting, read on with our piece on the great winter landscapes.
