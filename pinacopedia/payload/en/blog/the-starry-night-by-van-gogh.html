The Starry Night: what Van Gogh saw from his window

15 December 2026 · 6 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

The story of the world's most reproduced painting, made in an asylum in Saint-Rémy.

A sky swirling like a whirlpool, eleven huge stars, a crescent moon that looks like a sun and a black cypress rising like a flame. The Starry Night is probably the most reproduced painting in history: it is on mugs, T-shirts and screensavers. But behind that familiar image lies a more complex and moving story.

Painted from a barred window

In May 1889, after a serious crisis in Arles, Vincent van Gogh voluntarily entered the asylum of Saint-Paul-de-Mausole in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence. From his bedroom window he could see the fields and the Alpilles hills. He painted this view in June, from memory and by day, because he was not allowed to paint in his bedroom. The village with its steeple could not actually be seen from there: he invented it, and the tower looks more like a Dutch church than a Provençal one.

A real sky

Although it seems imaginary, the sky has something real about it. Astronomers have pointed out that Venus shone brightly before dawn at that time, and that the biggest star, next to the cypress, could be the planet. In his letters to his brother Theo, Vincent says he spent hours looking at the night sky from his window.

The cypress and death

In southern Europe cypresses are planted in cemeteries. Van Gogh wrote that he found them "beautiful as an Egyptian obelisk". In the painting it links earth and sky, and many read it as a symbol of death, but also of hope: for Vincent, the stars were a place you reached by dying, as you reach a town by train.

A painting he did not like

Curiously, Van Gogh did not consider it a success. In a letter he mentions it as a failed attempt at abstraction. He never sold it: as far as we know for certain, he sold only one painting in his lifetime. He died in July 1890, a year after painting it.

How it reached New York

After Theo's death, his widow Johanna van Gogh-Bonger devoted her life to making Vincent's work known. The painting passed through several collections until the Museum of Modern Art in New York acquired it in 1941, where it remains today.
