Feasts and parties in the history of painting

22 December 2026 · 6 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

From Bruegel's peasant wedding to Renoir's dances: how the masters painted great celebrations.

Christmas, New Year's Eve, Epiphany: weeks of long tables, music and gatherings. Painters have always been drawn to festivities, because they allow them to paint crowds, food, gestures and colour. Here is a short tour of the great banquets of art history.

Bruegel, The Peasant Wedding (1568)

In a barn, under a green cloth hung on the wall, sits the bride, hands folded and eyes closed. Nobody is quite sure where the groom is: he may be the one pouring from the jug, or one of the guests. Two men carry bowls of porridge on a door used as a tray, a child licks his fingers and a bagpiper stares hungrily at the food. Bruegel's Peasant Wedding is one of the liveliest scenes in sixteenth-century painting. Its companion is The Peasant Dance, where the party continues outdoors.

Veronese, The Wedding at Cana (1563)

In the Louvre, opposite the Mona Lisa, hangs the largest painting in the museum: almost ten metres wide. Paolo Veronese turned the miracle of water into wine into a Venetian banquet with more than a hundred guests, musicians, servants and dogs. Among the musicians in the centre, tradition recognises Veronese himself and other Venetian painters, such as Titian and Tintoretto.

Jan Steen, the party as chaos

In Holland people speak of "a Jan Steen household" to mean a messy home. The seventeenth-century painter portrayed family celebrations, such as the feast of St Nicholas, with children crying or laughing over their presents, adults drinking too much and cats stealing food. Behind the humour there is always a little moral lesson.

Renoir, Luncheon of the Boating Party (1881)

Renoir painted his friends eating and chatting on the terrace of a restaurant by the Seine. It is a party with no special occasion, just the pleasure of being together on a summer afternoon. One of the young women, the one playing with a little dog, is Aline Charigot, who later became his wife.

An idea for the holidays

If at your family meal there is someone who never stops serving, another who falls asleep after dessert and a child secretly eating the sweets, remember that Bruegel or Jan Steen would have painted them. And to carry on, look at the proverbs hidden in Netherlandish Proverbs.
