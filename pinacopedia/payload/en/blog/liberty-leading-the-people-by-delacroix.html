Liberty Leading the People: the story of a symbol

12 January 2027 · 6 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

How a painting about three days of revolution in Paris became the emblem of France.

A bare-breasted woman in a Phrygian cap strides over a barricade, the tricolour in one hand and a musket in the other. Beside her, a boy with two pistols and a man in a top hat. Liberty Leading the People has become the symbol of France and of revolutions all over the world.

Three days in July

Eugène Delacroix painted the picture in the autumn of 1830, a few months after the "Three Glorious Days", 27 to 29 July, when Parisians rose up against King Charles X and forced him to abdicate. Delacroix did not fight on the barricades, but he wrote to his brother: "if I have not fought for my country, at least I will paint for her".

A goddess and real men

Liberty is an allegory, an idealised figure like an ancient statue, but she is surrounded by very real characters: a worker with a sabre, a bourgeois in a frock coat and top hat, students and corpses in the foreground. That mixture of symbol and reality is what makes the painting so powerful. In the background, through the smoke, are the towers of Notre-Dame.

The boy who inspired Victor Hugo

The boy with the pistols, to Liberty's right, is a Paris street urchin. Many believe he inspired Victor Hugo's character Gavroche in Les Misérables, published in 1862.

An awkward painting

The new king, Louis-Philippe, bought the work in 1831, but it soon seemed too revolutionary and was kept out of public view for years. It only entered the Louvre for good in 1874.

A symbol that lives on

The figure of Liberty has inspired stamps, hundred-franc notes and, it is said, the Statue of Liberty in New York. In 2024 the painting was restored and recovered colours that yellowed varnish had dulled: the blue, white and red of the flag shine again.
