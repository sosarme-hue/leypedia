The Garden of Earthly Delights: a guide to getting lost in Bosch

1 December 2026 · 7 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

How to explore the Prado's most enigmatic triptych, panel by panel, without missing a detail.

It is one of those pictures where you can spend hours and keep finding things: naked men inside giant fruit, birds the size of horses, musical instruments turned into instruments of torture. Hieronymus Bosch's Garden of Earthly Delights, in the Prado Museum, is a whole world. Here is a route so you do not get lost.

A triptych that opens

The painting is a triptych: a central panel and two wings that close like doors. Closed, it shows the creation of the world in shades of grey, a transparent sphere with the Earth still without human beings. Open it, and colour explodes. It reads from left to right, like a story in three chapters.

Left: Paradise

God presents Eve to Adam in a garden full of real and fantastic animals: a giraffe, an elephant, a unicorn. It seems a peaceful scene, but look closely and there are unsettling details: a cat carrying off a mouse, animals devouring each other by the pond. Evil is already there.

Centre: the garden of pleasures

The central panel teems with hundreds of naked figures playing, embracing, eating giant strawberries and riding animals around a pool. It is a world of pleasure without apparent guilt. The most common interpretation is that it shows humanity given over to sin, especially lust, and that the fruits symbolise pleasures as sweet as they are fleeting.

Right: Hell

The last panel is dark and musical. A harp, a lute and a hurdy-gurdy are used to torture the damned; a bird-headed monster devours sinners while seated on a chamber-pot chair; and in the centre is the famous "tree man", a hollow body with a face looking back that many consider a self-portrait. On the backside of one of the damned there is even a musical score, which a student transcribed and recorded in 2014.

A painting for a palace

Bosch painted it around 1490-1510, probably for the Nassau palace in Brussels, where it was shown to guests. Philip II, a great admirer of the painter, acquired it and took it to the monastery of El Escorial in 1593. It has been in the Prado since 1939.

How to see it in the museum

Go early and stay at least twenty minutes. Start on the left, continue with the centre and finish in Hell, and look for specific details: an owl, a couple inside a bubble, a skater on a frozen river. If you want a method, try our ten minutes for looking at a painting.
