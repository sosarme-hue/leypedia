Persephone and the origin of the seasons

24 November 2026 · 5 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

An abduction, a desperate mother and a few pomegranate seeds: how the Greeks explained winter.

Why are there seasons? The ancient Greeks had an answer full of feeling: a mother who loses her daughter, and a daughter who has to divide her life between two worlds. This is the myth of Persephone, and autumn is the best time to tell it.

The abduction

The oldest account is the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, from the seventh century BC. Persephone, daughter of Demeter, the goddess of grain, was picking flowers in a meadow with her friends. As she reached for a narcissus of extraordinary beauty, the earth opened and Hades, lord of the underworld, appeared in his chariot. He seized her and carried her off to his kingdom below the earth to make her his wife. Zeus, the girl's father, had agreed to it.

A desperate mother

Demeter searched for her daughter for nine days with burning torches. When she learned what had happened, she left Olympus, disguised herself as an old woman and wandered among mortals. In her grief she stopped making the seeds grow: the fields dried up and humankind was close to starving. The gods, who no longer received offerings, had to step in.

The pomegranate seeds

Zeus sent Hermes to fetch Persephone. Hades let her go, but first he gave her pomegranate seeds to eat. Whoever tasted the food of the underworld was bound to it for ever. So every year Persephone would have to return to Hades: part of the year she would spend below the earth and the rest with her mother.

The cycle of the seasons

While Persephone is with Hades, Demeter mourns and the earth bears no fruit: that is winter. When her daughter returns, the mother rejoices and everything blooms: that is spring. Persephone thus became the queen of the underworld, respected and feared, and a symbol of life that dies and is reborn.

The Mysteries of Eleusis

At Eleusis, near Athens, secret rites were held in honour of the two goddesses, the Eleusinian Mysteries, for almost two thousand years. Initiates were forbidden to reveal what they saw, and to this day we do not know for sure what took place. The Romans called the goddess Proserpina, and Bernini carved her abduction in a marble famous for the way Pluto's hand sinks into the girl's skin.
