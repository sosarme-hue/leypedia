The Pleiades, seven sisters in the winter sky

8 December 2026 · 5 min read · By Alejandro Rosàs

The daughters of Atlas, pursued by Orion, shine on December nights. Myths from around the world.

On winter nights, looking east in the early evening and almost overhead at midnight, you can see a small, tight group of stars, like a pinch of glitter. These are the Pleiades, one of the sights in the sky that has inspired the most myths around the world.

The seven daughters of Atlas

For the Greeks they were seven sisters, daughters of the Titan Atlas and the nymph Pleione: Maia, Electra, Taygete, Alcyone, Celaeno, Sterope and Merope. Maia was the mother of Hermes, and other sisters had children by gods and founded royal lines.

Pursued by Orion

The giant hunter Orion fell in love with them and chased them for years. Zeus took pity on them and turned them first into doves and then into stars. But Orion also ended up in the sky, and even today his constellation seems to chase them night after night, always behind, never catching up.

The lost Pleiad

With the naked eye, most people can make out only six stars, not seven. The Greeks explained this with a myth: Merope married a mortal, the cunning Sisyphus, and is so ashamed that she hides her light. Another version says it is Electra, who covered her face in grief when she saw Troy fall, the city of her descendants.

A calendar in the sky

Hesiod, in Works and Days, advised farmers to start the harvest when the Pleiades rose at dawn, in early summer, and to plough when they set, in late autumn. Their appearance and disappearance set the rhythm of work in the fields and of sailing.

The Pleiades around the world

In Japan they are called Subaru, and they appear in the logo of the car maker of the same name. For the Māori of New Zealand they are Matariki, and their rising in midwinter marks the start of the new year, now a public holiday. Many Indigenous peoples of the Americas and Australia also see them as a group of sisters.

What they really are

The Pleiades are a cluster of hundreds of young, very hot stars, about 440 light-years away in the constellation of Taurus. With binoculars you can see dozens of them. December is one of the best months to look for them.
