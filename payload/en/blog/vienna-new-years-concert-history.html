The Vienna New Year's Concert: history and curiosities

The Strauss family, the 1939 origins of the concert, its great conductors and the rituals of the Blue Danube and the Radetzky March.

By Alejandro Rosàs Navarro

Share:FacebookWhatsApp

At noon on 1 January, tens of millions of people around the world switch on the television to watch the Vienna Philharmonic play waltzes and polkas in the Golden Hall of the Musikverein. The New Year's Concert is probably the most watched classical concert on the planet, and behind its festive air lies a long history, with lights and shadows, and a family of musicians who got the whole of Europe dancing: the Strausses.

A family that set Europe dancing

It all begins in the Vienna of the 1820s, when the waltz moved from popular dance halls to grand balls. Two young violinists and bandleaders competed for the public's favour: Joseph Lanner and Johann Strauss I. Strauss I also wrote the Radetzky March (1848), the piece that closes every New Year's Concert today.

His son, Johann Strauss II, took the waltz to its peak and earned the nickname "the Waltz King". His brothers followed: Josef Strauss, an engineer by training and perhaps the most poetic of the three, who wrote waltzes such as Sphärenklänge ("Music of the Spheres"), and Eduard Strauss, who led the family orchestra for decades. In 1907 Eduard burned much of the orchestra's music archive, including the family's scores and arrangements, a loss that researchers still regret.

An uncomfortable beginning: 1939

The concert as we know it was born on 31 December 1939, in a Vienna annexed by Nazi Germany and already at war. It was conducted by Clemens Krauss, with a programme devoted entirely to Johann Strauss II. From 1941 it moved to 1 January. The Vienna Philharmonic itself has studied and acknowledged this origin as part of its review of its history under Nazism, and it is worth remembering when we listen to such joyful music today.

After the war the concert continued and became a symbol of a Vienna that wanted to rebuild itself through its music.

Boskovsky, the conductor with a violin in his hand

From 1955 to 1979 it was led by Willi Boskovsky, the orchestra's concertmaster. He did it the way the Strausses themselves had done: standing in front of the orchestra, violin in hand, playing and conducting at the same time. Television broadcasts arrived with him, and the concert began to be seen outside Austria.

Since 1980 a different guest conductor has led it each year. Lorin Maazel conducted it seven years in a row, from 1980 to 1986. Herbert von Karajan conducted it only once, in 1987, with the soprano Kathleen Battle singing the waltz Voices of Spring. Carlos Kleiber, the most elusive of the great conductors, led it in 1989 and 1992, and for many music lovers his two concerts are the finest in its history. Zubin Mehta, Riccardo Muti, Nikolaus Harnoncourt and Mariss Jansons have also stood on the podium, among many others. In 2021, because of the pandemic, Muti conducted it to an empty hall, and the audience applauded from home over the internet.

The rituals everyone waits for

  • The false start of the Blue Danube. After the official programme come the encores. When the first shimmering bars of The Blue Danube are heard, the audience applauds, the orchestra stops, and the conductor and musicians wish the whole world a happy new year. Then the waltz begins for real.
  • Clapping along to the Radetzky March. The last piece is always Strauss I's march, and the audience claps along following the conductor, who "conducts" the hall as well.
  • The surprises. In many years the orchestra allows itself musical jokes: musicians who sing, whistle or make noises. The Pizzicato Polka, written jointly by Johann and Josef, and the Blacksmith Polka, with an anvil on stage, are classics.

The Blue Danube: a hit that started lukewarm

Today it seems that The Blue Danube was always a hit, but it was not. Johann Strauss II wrote it in 1867 as a choral waltz for a Viennese men's choral society, with words that convinced nobody. The orchestral version, presented the same year at the Paris World's Fair, turned it into a phenomenon. Since then it has been almost a second national anthem for Austria.

How to watch it and what to listen to next

Tickets are allocated by lottery among thousands of applications from all over the world, so the easiest way is to watch it on television: it is broadcast live in more than ninety countries. If you want more, try Kleiber's recordings from 1989 and 1992, or Boskovsky's albums, which capture the spirit of the Strausses' Vienna like few others.
