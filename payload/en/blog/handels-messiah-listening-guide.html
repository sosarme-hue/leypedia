Handel's Messiah: a listening guide for Christmas

History, key moments and recommended recordings of Handel's most famous oratorio, from the Dublin premiere to the Hallelujah chorus.

By Alejandro Rosàs Navarro

Share:FacebookWhatsApp

Every December, in hundreds of churches and concert halls around the world, a choir rises to its feet and sings "Hallelujah!". Few works of classical music have earned such a fixed place in the calendar as Messiah by George Frideric Handel. Yet it was first performed in spring, it was intended for Easter, and its first audience was not in London but in Dublin. This year, try listening to it with fresh ears: knowing where it comes from, what to listen for in each part and which recording to choose.

A work written in three weeks

In the summer of 1741 Handel was going through a difficult time. Italian opera, which had brought him fame and money in London, no longer filled the theatres, and the composer had debts and some health problems. Then he received a text from Charles Jennens, a cultivated landowner and music lover who had worked with him before. Jennens did not write a story with characters, as in Handel's other oratorios. Instead he selected verses from the King James Bible and the Psalms to tell, in three parts, the prophecy and birth of Christ, his passion and resurrection, and the final redemption.

Handel set the text between 22 August and 14 September 1741: about three weeks for almost two and a half hours of music. The speed has an explanation, because he reused some of his own material. The chorus "For unto us a child is born", for example, comes from an Italian duet he had written shortly before. This was normal practice at the time and takes nothing away from the work: the astonishing thing is how well these pieces fit the English words, as if they had been written for them.

Dublin, 13 April 1742

The first performance took place in the Great Music Hall in Fishamble Street, Dublin, at a charity concert for prisoners held for debt and for two of the city's hospitals. Demand was so high that the organisers asked ladies not to wear hooped skirts and gentlemen to leave their swords at home, so that more people could fit into the hall. Around seven hundred people are said to have attended.

Among the soloists was the actress and contralto Susannah Cibber, then at the centre of a much-discussed marital scandal. When she sang the aria "He was despised", a clergyman in the audience is said to have stood up and exclaimed: "Woman, for this be all thy sins forgiven thee." The story may be embellished, but it shows how the music moved its audience from the very first day.

London: from a cool reception to a tradition

In London, in 1743, the reception was cooler. Some people felt it was improper to sing a sacred text in a theatre. This is where another legend begins: that King George II stood up during the "Hallelujah" chorus, which is why audiences still stand today. No document confirms it, but the custom has survived for almost three centuries.

What really established the work were the charity performances Handel conducted from 1750 at the Foundling Hospital, a London orphanage. Messiah was performed there every year, and every year it raised money for the children. After the composer's death it was never out of the repertoire, and it was performed with ever larger forces: in 1784, at the Handel Commemoration in Westminster Abbey, hundreds of musicians took part, and in the nineteenth century the Crystal Palace festivals brought together thousands of singers.

Even Mozart worked on it. In 1789 he prepared a German version for Baron Gottfried van Swieten, with a richer woodwind section suited to Viennese taste. It is worth hearing at least once as a curiosity: it is Messiah seen through the eyes of the Classical era.

How to listen: a guide to the key moments

You do not need to follow the text word by word. Just listen out for a few key moments:

  • The Sinfony. A French overture: a slow, solemn opening with dotted rhythms, followed by a fast section in fugal style. From the first bar Handel tells us that what follows is serious.
  • "Comfort ye" and "Every valley". The tenor opens with words of comfort, then draws with his voice the valleys being raised and the mountains made low. A fine example of how Handel "paints" the words.
  • "For unto us a child is born". The nativity chorus, light and joyful, bursts out with the child's titles: "Wonderful, Counsellor, the Mighty God".
  • The Pifa, or Pastoral Symphony. A short orchestral piece evoking the shepherds. Handel was inspired by the pifferari, pipers who came down from the mountains to Rome at Christmas, whom he had heard as a young man in Italy.
  • "He was despised". The longest and perhaps the deepest aria in the work, the one that moved Dublin to tears.
  • "Hallelujah". It closes Part Two, not the whole work. Listen to how the choir alternates block chords with imitative entries, and how the trumpets and timpani join for the grand finale.
  • "I know that my Redeemer liveth" and the final "Amen". The soprano opens Part Three with luminous serenity, and the work ends with a great fugue on a single word: "Amen".

Which recording to choose

There are hundreds of recordings of Messiah, and each reflects a period and a way of understanding Handel's music. Here are four very different ways to hear it:

  • The grand one: Thomas Beecham recorded a version in 1959 with an expanded orchestration, cymbals included. It is far from what Handel heard, but irresistible as a document of the Romantic taste for the monumental.
  • The transitional one: Colin Davis recorded a leaner, lighter Messiah in 1966, just as the world was beginning to rethink how Baroque music should sound.
  • The historically informed ones: Christopher Hogwood reconstructed the Foundling Hospital version on period instruments, and John Eliot Gardiner offered an energetic, transparent reading with his English Baroque Soloists.
  • The theatrical one: William Christie and Les Arts Florissants bring out the dramatic side of a work by a composer who came from the opera house.

If this is your first time, start with a period-instrument version: the tempos are livelier, the choir is smaller and the lines are clearer. Then go back to Beecham to understand why the nineteenth century loved this work so passionately.

Why at Christmas

Messiah was first performed at Easter, and only its first part tells of the nativity. But the prophecy, the shepherds and the angels' "Glory to God" are so close to Christmas that tradition, especially in the English-speaking world, ended up moving it to December. Today it is as much a part of Christmas as the tree or carols, and many cities even hold sing-along concerts in which the audience joins in the choruses.
