Bach's Christmas Oratorio: a part-by-part listening guide

Six cantatas for six feast days: what to listen for in each, the recycled music and the best recordings.

By Alejandro Rosàs Navarro

Share:FacebookWhatsApp

In our list of twelve works for Christmas, Bach's Christmas Oratorio held a special place. It deserves a guide of its own, because it is a long work, almost three hours, and it is far more enjoyable if you know how it is built and what to listen for in each part.

Six cantatas for six feast days

Bach did not write it to be heard in one sitting. It consists of six cantatas first performed in Leipzig's two main churches, St Thomas and St Nicholas, on the feast days of the winter of 1734–35: Christmas Day, the second and third days of Christmas, New Year's Day, the following Sunday and Epiphany, 6 January. Each tells part of the story, so the oratorio accompanied the congregation through the whole season.

Recycled music, and very well recycled

Bach reused music from several secular cantatas he had written shortly before to celebrate the birthdays and name days of the Saxon Elector's family. The famous opening chorus, for example, comes from a cantata for the Queen's birthday that began with the words "Sound, you drums! Ring out, trumpets!", which explains why the oratorio opens with a timpani solo. The text is attributed to the poet Picander, Bach's regular collaborator. This kind of recycling was common practice and allowed music written for a single occasion to enjoy a much longer life.

A guide, part by part

  • I. The birth. Timpani, trumpets and the chorus "Jauchzet, frohlocket!". Listen also to the chorale "Wie soll ich dich empfangen", sung to the melody Bach used in the St Matthew Passion for "O Haupt voll Blut und Wunden". Already at Christmas, the Passion is foreshadowed.
  • II. The shepherds. It opens with a pastoral sinfonia in which two groups converse: strings and flutes, representing the angels, and the rustic-sounding oboes da caccia, representing the shepherds. It is one of Bach's most beautiful instrumental pages. It also contains a tender lullaby for alto, "Schlafe, mein Liebster".
  • III. The adoration of the shepherds. Shorter and more intimate, with a mystical love duet for soprano and bass. It ends by repeating its own opening chorus.
  • IV. New Year. The trumpets fall silent and hunting horns sound. Look out for the echo aria for soprano, in which a second soprano in the distance answers "yes" or "no" to the soul's questions.
  • V. The Magi. The Wise Men arrive in Jerusalem asking where the King of the Jews is, and Herod grows uneasy. There is a beautiful trio for soprano, alto and tenor.
  • VI. Epiphany. The trumpets return for the most brilliant finale. The last chorale again uses the Passion melody, but now in a mood of victory, with trumpets and timpani. The oratorio begins and ends with the same theme, transformed.

Which recording to choose

  • The classic: Karl Richter and his Munich choir and orchestra recorded a solemn, monumental version in 1965, the reference for decades.
  • The period-instrument pioneers: Nikolaus Harnoncourt, with boys singing the soprano and alto parts, and John Eliot Gardiner, brighter and faster.
  • The more recent ones: Philippe Herreweghe, Ton Koopman, Masaaki Suzuki and René Jacobs offer transparent readings with small choirs and great clarity.

A suggestion for the holidays

Do as the congregation in Leipzig did in 1734: listen to the first cantata on Christmas Day, the second on the 26th, the third on the 27th, the fourth on 1 January, the fifth on the following Sunday and the sixth on Epiphany. That way the oratorio will accompany you through the whole season, just as Bach intended.
