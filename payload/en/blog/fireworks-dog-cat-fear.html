Fireworks: how to help your dog or cat cope with the fear

New Year's Eve, Bonfire Night and local festivals: for many dogs and cats, fireworks mean the worst night of the year. What to do before, during and after, and what to avoid.

Key points
  • Fear of loud noises is very common and is not just being difficult.
  • Walk your dog before the fireworks start, and always on a lead.
  • Close windows and curtains and prepare a quiet hiding place.
  • You can stay with your pet and comfort them calmly: it does not reinforce the fear.
  • Never punish them or force them to face the noise.
  • If the fear is intense, talk to your vet weeks in advance.

Why they are so frightened

Dogs and cats hear sounds that we cannot and are very sensitive to loud, unpredictable noises. Fireworks are sudden, their source is unclear and they often come with flashes and the smell of gunpowder. For many animals they are a real threat.

Signs of fear can be obvious, such as trembling, panting, hiding, trying to escape, or barking or meowing constantly, or more subtle: lip licking, yawning, ears pulled back, tail tucked between the legs or refusing to eat. Cats most often hide and stay out of sight for hours.

Before fireworks night

Walk your dog early, while it is still light, so that they can relieve themselves and burn off energy. Always use a lead, even if they are usually off-lead: a firework can make them bolt without looking. Check that their ID tag is secure and their microchip details are up to date.

Prepare a hiding place in the quietest room of the house, away from windows: their bed, a blanket, water and a toy. Many dogs like an open crate covered with a blanket, and cats appreciate a high spot or a box to hide in. Let them get used to it for a few days beforehand.

Feed them a while before the noise starts, because many frightened animals will not eat.

During the fireworks

Close windows, blinds and curtains to muffle the noise and the flashes. A television, radio or calm music at a moderate volume helps to mask the bangs.

Stay at home if you can. If your pet seeks your company, stay with them and talk to them calmly: comforting a frightened animal does not reinforce its fear. If they prefer to hide, let them, and do not pull them out of their hiding place.

Try to stay calm yourself and carry on with what you were doing. Some dogs can be distracted with a food puzzle toy or something to chew.

What to avoid

Do not punish or scold your pet for being afraid, barking or having an accident: it only increases their distress. Do not force them outside or towards the noise so they get used to it; that usually makes the fear worse.

Do not leave them alone on a balcony, in the garden or in the car, and do not give them human sedatives or medicines: some are dangerous for dogs and cats.

In the long term

Noise fear can be worked on over time. Desensitisation means playing recordings of fireworks at a very low volume while your pet is doing something pleasant, such as eating or playing, and increasing it very gradually over weeks. A trainer or behaviourist can help you do it properly.

If the fear is intense, if your pet hurts itself or tries to escape, talk to your vet several weeks in advance. They can assess whether some treatment, pheromone diffusers or other aids are advisable, and when to start.

When to see a vet

See your vet if the fear is intense or gets worse each year, if your pet injures itself trying to escape, if it does not eat for more than a day after the noise or if it is still very distressed the next day. Book an appointment weeks before New Year's Eve or your local festivities, not on the day.

General guidance. It does not replace a visit to your vet.
