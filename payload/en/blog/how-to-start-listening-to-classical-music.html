How to start listening to classical music: tips and 10 works

Five practical tips and ten works, from Vivaldi to Ravel, for anyone who wants to start listening to classical music this year.

By Alejandro Rosàs Navarro

Share:FacebookWhatsApp

Many people tell me they would like to listen to classical music but do not know where to start. That is natural: there are a thousand years of music, thousands of composers and dozens of recordings of every work. I started when I was twelve and I still discover new things every week. If this is the year you want to begin, here are some practical tips and ten works for your first steps.

Five tips before you begin

  • Start with short works. There is no need to begin with an hour-long symphony. A ten-minute movement is enough to know whether you like something.
  • Listen again. Classical music gains a great deal from repeated listening. What seems confusing the first time becomes familiar by the third and indispensable by the fifth.
  • Listen attentively, at least once. It works as background music, but the experience changes completely if you give it your full attention for a while, with good headphones.
  • Go to a concert. Nothing replaces the sound of a live orchestra. Many cities have affordable series, open rehearsals or concerts for young people.
  • Follow the thread. If you like a work, look up the composer on Classicapedia, read the biography and listen to other works by them, or by their teachers and pupils. That is how you build your own taste.

Ten works to start with

  1. Vivaldi: The Four Seasons. Music that tells stories of birds, storms and ice. We explain it in our guide to The Four Seasons.
  2. Bach: Brandenburg Concerto No. 3. Pure energy in under fifteen minutes, with a string orchestra that sounds like a perfect machine.
  3. Mozart: Piano Concerto No. 21. The second movement, the "Andante", is one of the most beautiful melodies ever written.
  4. Beethoven: Symphony No. 7. Rather than the Fifth, which everyone knows, try the Seventh: rhythmic, radiant and with a hypnotic second movement.
  5. Chopin: Nocturnes. Short, intimate, singing piano pieces, perfect for the evening.
  6. Grieg: Peer Gynt. "Morning Mood" and "In the Hall of the Mountain King" will sound familiar even if you do not know where from.
  7. Tchaikovsky: Piano Concerto No. 1. Overwhelming opening chords and Romantic melodies from start to finish.
  8. Dvořák: "New World" Symphony. Written in the United States with nostalgia for Bohemia. The "Largo" is unforgettable.
  9. Debussy: Clair de lune and Prelude to the Afternoon of a Faun. Music becomes colour, light and mist.
  10. Ravel: Boléro. A single repeated melody and a fifteen-minute crescendo. A masterclass in orchestration.

And then

Once these works feel familiar, try something longer: a Puccini opera such as La bohème, Mozart's Requiem or the symphonies of Gustav Mahler, which are whole worlds. And do not worry about "understanding". Nobody needs to read music to be moved by it. All you have to do is listen.
